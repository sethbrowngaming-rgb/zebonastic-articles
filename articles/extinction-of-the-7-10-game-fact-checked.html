The Extinction of the "7/10" Game: How Subscriptions, Backlogs, and Market Polarization Reshaped Mid-Budget Gaming Economics

A Game Research Writer (GRW) analysis. Claim-by-claim sourcing is documented in the accompanying claim ledger. Several figures in earlier drafts of this piece could not be verified and have been corrected or removed below.

1. Introduction

The interactive entertainment industry has historically drawn commercial stability from its mid-budget sector — the tier of games colloquially known, half-affectionately, as the "7/10 game" or "AA tier." Built on moderate budgets and compressed schedules, these titles traded blockbuster scale for a focused mechanical idea, a niche narrative concept, or a specific license, and they turned a profit at mid-range prices rather than at $70 with a nine-figure marketing campaign behind them.

That middle tier is now under severe pressure, and the evidence for why is unusually well documented, because much of it comes from sources that were never meant to be public: leaked internal valuations, court filings, and a sworn declaration. In September 2026, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney described the industry's current contraction as its most severe disruption since the 1983 console crash, in a statement covered by outlets including Kotaku and Tech4Gamers. Sweeney's own words, as reported, were narrower than the historical comparison suggests: "an unexpected, severe disruption," driven by the fact that "prices of RAM and storage are quadrupling, and not necessarily stopping there." That distinction matters. The 1983 crash was a crisis of oversupply and low quality. What Sweeney is describing is closer to the opposite problem — games that already cost too much and take too long to build, now facing a hardware-component shock on top of it.

This analysis asks a narrower question than "is the industry crashing": what does the available evidence say happened, specifically, to the mid-budget game — and does it support the idea that subscriptions, shrinking attention, and price polarization squeezed it out? The short answer is that the evidence supports real and measurable pressure on that tier, but not every popular explanation for it holds up equally well once checked against primary sources, and a few specific figures that circulate in commentary on this topic — including in an earlier draft of this piece — turn out to be unsupported or simply wrong.

2. Context: A Cost Shock Layered on an Existing Squeeze

Component costs are the newest and most concrete pressure. Independent market reporting (Wccftech, citing IDC-style industry tracking) documents consumer RAM prices rising by as much as 110% and SSD prices by roughly 147% in the first quarter of 2026 alone, on top of a prior surge of more than 90% — driven by AI data centers' demand for high-bandwidth memory, which requires roughly three times the wafer capacity per bit compared to standard DDR5. This is a documented OBSERVATION, not a inference: multiple independent trackers converge on the same driver and a similar order of magnitude, and Sweeney's own "quadrupling" figure is consistent with it.

That cost shock has reached console hardware directly. Sony raised the PS5 Pro from $749.99 to $899.99 effective April 2, 2026, citing "continued pressures in the global economic landscape." Microsoft followed on August 1, 2026, raising the Xbox Series X 1TB disc model from $649.99 to $799.99 and explicitly attributing the increase to "pressure from RAM shortages." Six years into the console generation, the traditional pattern of hardware price decay has reversed. That restricts organic console-install-base growth — a mechanical constraint on the primary channel through which mid-budget console games reach new players.

Layered on top of that is an industry contraction already several years old. A widely used aggregated tracker (compiled from layoffs.fyi, Aftermath, and primary reporting, hosted on Wikipedia) puts total industry job losses at approximately 45,000 between 2022 and mid-2025, with 2024 as the single worst year at roughly 15,650 positions, and more than 30 studios closed outright. Different trackers produce somewhat different cumulative totals depending on methodology and cutoff date, and this research did not find a single authoritative figure specifically updated through mid-2026 — though continued 2026 reporting, including Sweeney's own statement, indicates the contraction has not reversed. The figure is best read as "45,000-plus and still climbing," not as a precisely audited number frozen at a specific date.

Separately, AAA development costs for some flagship titles have been confirmed, through court filings, a sworn legal declaration, and one hacked internal document cache, at figures exceeding $300 million per game — Marvel's Spider-Man 2 near $300 million (Insomniac Games leak) and Call of Duty: Black Ops Cold War over $700 million in lifecycle cost (Activision's own sworn court declaration). These are real, disclosed figures for specific titles, not a stated industry-wide average, and this piece treats them as such — this reflects the constraint faced by a small number of disclosed titles, not a confirmed universal AAA cost floor.

None of these pressures directly explains why the mid-budget "7/10" tier specifically has struggled, as opposed to the industry broadly. The next two sections take up the two mechanisms most directly implicated: where player attention actually goes, and how subscription economics change what a mid-tier release is worth.

3. Evidence: The Attention Economy and the Backlog Squeeze

Newzoo's PC & Console Gaming Report, presented at GDC 2025 and covering 2024 data (a survey of 73,000 gamers across more than 10,000 titles), found that PC gamers spent 67% of their playtime on games six or more years old, 25% on games two to five years old, and just 8% on releases under two years old. That finding should carry one methodological caveat: Newzoo has previously revised comparable figures for an adjacent year downward after updating its own methodology — a prior-year "6+ years old" estimate moved from 61% to 55% on revision. The directional finding — that a large majority of PC playtime goes to older titles, and new releases capture a small share — is well corroborated across multiple Newzoo releases; the exact percentage-point figures carry real measurement uncertainty that the source's own revision history makes explicit.

A related, separately sourced Newzoo finding is more specific and easier to verify: in 2023, just 66 individual titles accounted for 80% of all global playtime, a concentration Newzoo analyst Tom Wijman attributed largely to a handful of long-running live-service and sandbox platforms — Fortnite, Roblox, Minecraft, Grand Theft Auto V, and Call of Duty are the examples named in that reporting. Separately, aggregate quarterly playtime across PC and console fell 26% from its early-2021 pandemic-era peak, per the same Newzoo report line, corroborated by Reuters wire reporting.

This concentration creates a specific problem for the mid-tier release, and it is best stated as an INTERPRETATION rather than a proven mechanism: when a typical player has a shrinking pool of total playtime and a growing backlog of already-owned, already-discounted, critically vetted older titles competing for it, a new $70 purchase requiring a 30-to-40-hour commitment has to clear a higher bar than it did when backlogs were smaller and attention was less concentrated in a handful of live-service platforms. A critical score of "7 out of 10" — competent, not broken, but not exceptional — is, under these conditions, a weaker purchase signal than it would have been a decade earlier, because "not broken" no longer distinguishes a new release from the enormous library of already-vetted older games sitting in a player's backlog or subscription library. Available sources document the concentration of playtime and the shrinking new-release share; they do not directly measure how much of any specific game's underperformance is attributable to this dynamic versus quality, marketing, or genre choice — that causal weighting is not something this dataset can isolate.

4. Evidence: The Subscription Paradox

Multi-game subscription services were pitched, in effect, as television's model applied to games: a platform holder needs a steady stream of content to retain subscribers, and mid-budget single-player titles fit neatly between multi-year AAA development cycles. Two categories of evidence — a 2023 leak of Microsoft's own internal Game Pass valuations, and a 2023 UK Competition and Markets Authority filing — show what that model actually costs platform holders and what it does to direct sales.

Leaked 2020-dated Microsoft documents, reported by VGC, Game Developer, Axios, and Kotaku following a 2023 leak connected to the FTC v. Microsoft litigation, show the following confirmed internal valuations for bringing third-party titles to Game Pass day-and-date:

Title Estimated Valuation Outcome
Star Wars Jedi: Survivor $300 million Deal not completed; EA kept it off Game Pass
Mortal Kombat 1 $250 million Deal not completed
Assassin's Creed Mirage $100 million Anticipated Ubisoft ask, per leak
Dying Light 2 $50 million Anticipated developer ask, per leak
Baldur's Gate 3 $5 million Microsoft's own valuation — later acknowledged by Larian's Swen Vincke as a significant underestimate given the game's eventual success
Grand Theft Auto V $12–15 million per month Recurring licensing fee, with internal uncertainty about renewal beyond 3–4 months

An earlier version of this table also listed valuations for Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League, Gotham Knights, and Lego Star Wars: The Skywalker Saga. Those three figures could not be traced to any source in this research pass and have been removed rather than repeated as fact — a direct application of this analysis's evidence-gap principle. The Dying Light 2 figure, which is confirmed, has been substituted where a mid-range valuation example was needed.

The February 2023 CMA filing adds a second, independently damaging data point: Microsoft's own submissions to the regulator acknowledged that adding a title to Game Pass "severely cannibalizes" its direct buy-to-play sales, with the specific percentage redacted in the public filing. Because this is an admission by the party with every incentive to minimize the effect, it is treated here as strong, confirmed evidence rather than a claim requiring further corroboration.

That combination — high upfront valuations for a shrinking number of marquee deals, plus a self-acknowledged cannibalization effect on direct sales — is the core of the paradox. Subscriptions can de-risk an individual release with a large guaranteed payment, but they simultaneously train subscribers to expect games to arrive "for free" as part of a monthly fee, weakening the case for an unproven mid-budget title to ask full price outside the subscription.

Two executives have staked out opposing positions publicly and repeatedly. Take-Two CEO Strauss Zelnick has said, across multiple interviews over several years, that launching a new game into a subscription service on day one "doesn't make any sense" economically — a confirmed, repeated, on-record position, not a one-off remark. Larian Studios' Swen Vincke has separately and consistently expressed concern that a subscription-dominated future could be "savage" for developers and has stated Larian's own games won't go to subscription services; the framing that platform holders could become gatekeepers funding projects by retention-algorithm metrics rather than creative merit is a fair paraphrase of Vincke's general public position across several interviews, though it should be read as paraphrase rather than a single verbatim quotation.

Hi-Fi Rush is frequently cited in this debate as a cautionary case, and the confirmed facts support caution about how far to take it. Tango Gameworks' Hi-Fi Rush (2023) was a surprise Game Pass day-one release that was critically well received; Microsoft itself has described the game as a success. Tango Gameworks was nonetheless closed in 2024 as part of a broader wave of Xbox studio closures (alongside Alpha Dog Games and Arkane Austin), before Krafton — the South Korean publisher behind PUBG — acquired the studio and the Hi-Fi Rush IP in August 2024. What this research could not confirm is the specific causal chain sometimes attached to this story: that the closure happened because subscriber engagement failed to convert into direct sales or recurring revenue. No source locates a statement from Microsoft tying the closure to that specific mechanism, and the closure occurred alongside other studios whose games were not Game Pass exclusives. Per this analysis's causality test, the correct framing is that Hi-Fi Rush's fate is consistent with — not proof of — the risk that subscription success doesn't protect a studio, not a confirmed example of subscription economics causing a specific closure decision.

Cost pressure on the platform side itself is now visible too. In early September 2026, Microsoft announced monthly cloud-streaming hour caps for Game Pass — 15 hours for Ultimate, 10 for Premium, 5 for Essential subscribers, effective November 2026 — stating that cloud-compute costs scale with usage and session length, with additional hours purchasable beyond the cap. Microsoft said the change would affect roughly 4% of subscribers. This is a confirmed, very recent signal that platform holders are tightening the economics of the subscription model itself, which plausibly narrows the financial cushion mid-tier developers had been counting on — though it is too recent for any data yet to exist on its actual effect on developer deal terms.

5. Case Studies: Price, Scope, and Outcome

Four recent case studies illustrate a consistent pattern: mid-budget games that launched at the standard $70 AAA price without an established audience have struggled, while games that matched their price and marketing to a defined audience and a tighter scope have performed well — including at prices below $70.

Immortals of Aveum (Ascendant Studios, published by Electronic Arts under the EA Originals label) is the clearest failure case. Reporting citing former-developer accounts puts its total cost at roughly $125 million ($85 million development, $40 million marketing and distribution). It launched August 22, 2023, at $70, with an OpenCritic top-critic average of 72 — a genuinely "7/10" consensus score, though only 48% of critics recommended it, a figure that arguably tells a sharper story than the average score alone. Sales fell well short of internal projections, and Ascendant Studios laid off 45% of its staff on September 14, 2023, less than a month after launch. Former developers have characterized building an original, unproven IP at full AAA pricing as a flawed strategy; this analysis cannot independently verify that specific attribution beyond noting it is a recurring theme in coverage of the game's failure, since no source ties the layoffs to a single isolated cause with certainty.

RoboCop: Rogue City (Teyon, published by Nacon) is a confirmed success case at a moderate budget and a sub-$70 price point (reported around $50). It became Nacon's best-ever game launch, and Nacon's gaming division reported quarterly revenue rising to €59 million on the back of it — both figures confirmed across multiple outlets. This research could not independently confirm the specific "43% quarterly increase" figure cited in some coverage as a standalone number distinct from the €59 million headline, though the direction (a meaningful revenue increase, driven by the launch) is well supported.

Warhammer 40,000: Space Marine 2 (Saber Interactive, published by Focus Entertainment) sold over 4.5 million copies within its first month — confirmed by multiple outlets — and later surpassed 7 million. Saber CEO Matthew Karch has criticized standard $70 pricing on record, arguing publishers can "make great entertainment at a price that isn't going to kill somebody's bank account," and has pointed to Saber's own production efficiency as evidence: he states Saber's budget for Halo: The Master Chief Collection was roughly one-twentieth to one-fortieth of Halo Infinite's. An earlier version of this analysis attributed a specific budget comparison between Space Marine 2 and Doom Eternal to Karch; no source supports that specific comparison, and it has been removed. The verified evidence for Saber's cost-efficiency argument is the Halo comparison, not a Doom Eternal one, and Space Marine 2's own budget relative to other AAA shooters is not independently disclosed in available sources.

Helldivers 2 (Arrowhead Game Studios, published by Sony Interactive Entertainment) launched at $39.99 in February 2024. Sony's own FY23 earnings disclosure confirms over 12 million units sold within roughly three months — comfortably within its first year. Higher figures circulating later (19–20 million units, $700 million-plus in revenue) come from third-party analytics firm Alinea Analytics roughly two years after launch, not from Sony directly, and should not be blended with the confirmed first-year figure as if both describe the same measurement period.

6. Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations

The pattern above is suggestive, but it rests on four case studies chosen because their outcomes were unusually well documented — not a representative, randomly sampled set of mid-budget releases. A title's price point is also not independent of other factors that plausibly drive its outcome on its own: RoboCop and Space Marine 2 both carried recognizable licenses (RoboCop the film franchise, Warhammer 40,000 an established tabletop and video game IP with a large existing fanbase), while Immortals of Aveum was an entirely new IP with no pre-existing audience. It is equally plausible that brand recognition, not price alone, drove the difference in outcomes — a game with Immortals of Aveum's budget and no license may have struggled at $50 nearly as much as it did at $70, and this analysis's evidence cannot cleanly separate the two effects.

The subscription-cannibalization evidence (Section 4) is genuinely strong — it is a direct admission by Microsoft to a regulator — but it describes an effect at the level of an individual title added to Game Pass. It does not, on its own, establish that subscriptions are net harmful to the mid-budget tier as a whole; a title that would not have sold well at retail at all might still generate meaningful revenue through an upfront platform payment it would never have received otherwise, and several of the leaked valuations in Section 4 (Jedi Survivor, Mortal Kombat 1) describe deals publishers ultimately declined, precisely because they judged direct sales more valuable — meaning the market mechanism this analysis describes may already be self-correcting for well-established franchises, if not for unproven ones.

The attention-economy evidence (Section 3) faces its own competing explanation. Newzoo's own methodology revisions demonstrate that "share of playtime by game age" is genuinely difficult to measure precisely, and the report itself excludes China and Nintendo Switch — a substantial share of the global market, and one where different playtime patterns plausibly exist. A pure backlog/attention explanation for mid-tier struggles is also difficult to reconcile with the fact that some new, unproven releases (Helldivers 2, Space Marine 2) achieved massive playtime and sales despite the same backlog conditions — suggesting execution and positioning can substantially override the general trend rather than being determined by it.

Finally, this analysis's own framing invites a direct rebuttal: it presents rising component costs, subscription economics, and attention concentration as three separate pressures converging on the mid-tier, but available sources do not establish their relative weight against one another, or against a much simpler alternative explanation — that "mid-budget" games have always had a wide range of commercial outcomes, and that recent high-profile failures (Immortals of Aveum among them) are getting more attention now because the industry-wide layoff wave (Section 2) has made every individual failure more newsworthy, not because the underlying odds of failure for a well-executed mid-budget game have necessarily worsened.

7. Limitations of This Analysis

This piece relies on figures obtained through a mix of disclosure channels with very different reliability profiles — a platform holder's own regulatory admission (strong), leaked internal documents never intended for publication (moderate — real, but not independently audited), and industry trackers that aggregate primary reporting using inconsistent methodologies (the 45,000-jobs figure, the Game Pass valuation table). Newzoo's own downward revision of a comparable prior-year playtime statistic is a useful reminder that even reputable market-research figures carry real uncertainty and should not be treated as precise to the percentage point. Several specific figures that circulated in earlier coverage of this topic — the Suicide Squad, Gotham Knights, and Lego Star Wars Game Pass valuations; the Space Marine 2/Doom Eternal budget comparison; a direct causal link between Hi-Fi Rush's subscriber economics and Tango Gameworks' closure — could not be traced to any primary or credible secondary source and have been removed rather than presented as fact.

8. Conclusion

What we know: Component costs for RAM and storage have risen sharply in 2025–2026, driven by AI data-center demand, and this has pushed console hardware prices upward for the first time in a generation without the traditional post-launch price decay (PS5 Pro to $899.99, Xbox Series X to $799.99). Industry-wide layoffs have exceeded 45,000 since 2022, with more than 30 studios closed. PC playtime is heavily concentrated in older titles and a small number of live-service platforms, and total playtime has fallen 26% since its 2021 peak. Microsoft's own regulatory filings confirm that Game Pass inclusion cannibalizes direct sales, and the company has begun tightening subscription economics further via cloud-streaming caps introduced in 2026. Mid-budget titles that launched at the standard $70 price without an established audience (Immortals of Aveum) have failed commercially and led to significant layoffs, while titles that matched price to audience and license recognition (RoboCop: Rogue City, Space Marine 2, Helldivers 2) succeeded at a range of price points from $40 to $70.

What we think: The available evidence is consistent with — but does not conclusively prove — a model in which rising costs, shrinking and more concentrated player attention, and subscription economics are jointly squeezing the specific range of games that are too ambitious to be cheap indie titles but not distinctive or well-established enough to justify a $70 asking price without a recognizable license. Price point alone does not appear to determine outcomes; license recognition and audience-matched marketing appear at least as important in the four case studies examined, and this analysis cannot cleanly separate their relative contributions.

What we don't know: No source establishes the relative weight of component costs, subscription cannibalization, and attention concentration against one another, or against the simpler possibility that mid-budget commercial variance has always been wide and is now simply more visible amid a broader industry contraction. The specific causal mechanism behind Tango Gameworks' 2024 closure remains unconfirmed by any source tying it directly to Hi-Fi Rush's Game Pass performance. And whether the 2026 memory-price shock is a temporary shortage or a multi-year structural shift — Sweeney has suggested a three-year outlook — is not something historical data can answer in advance.
