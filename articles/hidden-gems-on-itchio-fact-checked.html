Hidden Gems on itch.io: What the Evidence Actually Shows

A Game Research Writer analysis. Compiled and fact-checked September 2026.

1. Introduction

"Hidden gem on itch.io" is one of the most repeated phrases in indie game culture, and one of the least examined. It shows up in YouTube titles, community collections, and press roundups, almost always as an unexamined compliment: itch.io, the story goes, is where real discovery still happens, away from Steam's algorithm and its wishlist thresholds.

This article does not attempt to hand the reader another top-ten list of games to try tonight. That kind of list is a taste claim, not a research finding, and dressing personal recommendations up as objective discovery would misrepresent what can actually be verified. Instead, this piece asks a narrower and more answerable question: when a game does travel from itch.io obscurity to real recognition, what does the documented record show about how that happens — and how much of itch.io's "hidden gem" reputation rests on a small number of well-covered outliers against a much larger, largely unmeasured backdrop of uploads that nobody ever finds?

Answering that means separating a few different kinds of claims that get blended together in casual discussion of the platform: itch.io's own disclosed facts about its scale and mechanics (strong evidence), independently documented case histories of specific games that did break out (strong evidence, case by case), and the folk theory that itch.io's system is what causes that breakout (much weaker evidence, and the part of the story this article treats most skeptically).

2. Context: A Platform Built Not to Curate

itch.io was built by a single developer, Leaf Corcoran, who launched the site on March 3, 2013 after building it as a side project. Its foundational design choice — one that still defines it — is that essentially anyone can publish anything, with no submission fee and no gatekeeping review, and that developers set their own revenue split with the platform, defaulting to 10% but adjustable to anywhere from 0% upward.

That openness was followed by rapid growth in catalog size, whatever the precise mix of causes. itch.io's own blog disclosed roughly 200,000 hosted games as of November 2019. Later reporting puts the figure at over 1,000,000 hosted products by November 2024, though this research could not locate itch.io's own announcement of that specific number — only a secondary citation of it — and "products" on itch.io includes non-game items (soundtracks, art packs, tools, and the flood of short game-jam entries the site also hosts), so the two figures are not cleanly comparable as a "games" count.

itch.io has been explicit that it does not solve discovery the way a recommendation algorithm would. In its own 2019 blog post addressing the problem directly — titled, with some self-awareness, "There are 200,000 games on itch.io. Here's how to find your new favorite" — the platform described its approach as built around human curation: featured and "fresh games" modules on the front page, weekly staff blog picks, a tagging system for browsing by genre or theme, the ability to follow individual creators for update alerts (which the post calls "the one I'm most surprised isn't more utilized"), and periodic themed "Selects" bundles. The post mentions algorithmic modules only in passing, not as the platform's primary discovery mechanism.

It is worth stating plainly what this research could and could not confirm about the present day. That 2019 post is the most detailed primary account of itch.io's discovery philosophy this research located. A separate itch.io post referencing a redesigned browse-and-search system indicates the site has continued to iterate since then, but this article did not find a current, equally detailed account of exactly how browsing and recommendation work on itch.io as of 2026. Where this piece describes itch.io's discovery mechanics, it is describing a documented 2019 baseline, not a confirmed current state — a gap the article treats as a gap rather than papering over with a plausible-sounding guess.

Two other structural facts matter for what follows. First, itch.io has run large charitable bundles that briefly turned mass discovery into mass transaction: the 2020 Bundle for Racial Justice and Equality — a pay-what-you-want-minimum package that itch.io's own bundle page credits to "itch.io and 1,391 others," and which grew past 1,500 individual items by the time contemporaneous community trackers stopped counting — raised approximately $8.1–8.2 million in about two weeks, according to contemporaneous reporting from TheGamer, Destructoid, and PC Gamer (the exact figure varies slightly by outlet and by the moment it was reported, since the bundle kept accepting funds briefly after its formal close). Later, smaller bundles for causes including Ukraine relief, abortion funds, and immigration-related causes raised sums in the low hundreds of thousands of dollars each, according to a tertiary aggregation on Wikipedia this research did not independently re-trace outlet by outlet. Second, itch.io's centralized position in the payment chain is also a point of fragility: in July 2025, the platform temporarily deindexed all games tagged as mature content platform-wide, in response to pressure tied to payment processors and a campaign by the Australian nonprofit Collective Shout, a move founder Leaf Corcoran attributed to protecting the site's payment infrastructure. Critics, including NYU Game Center chair Naomi Clark, argued the blanket response risked sweeping in legitimate games addressing serious adult themes alongside pornographic content — a reminder that a platform's discovery mechanics can be overridden overnight by concerns that have nothing to do with curation at all.

3. Evidence: What "Discovery" Actually Looked Like in Documented Breakout Cases

If itch.io's front page, tags, and bundles are not, by the platform's own account, doing algorithmic heavy lifting, the interesting question becomes: in cases where an itch.io game did become widely known, what actually carried it there? This section traces several well-documented cases to answer that as precisely as the record allows.

Doki Doki Literature Club is the clearest example. Dan Salvato, working as Team Salvato, released the game free (pay-what-you-want) on itch.io on September 22, 2017. Within three months it had passed 1 million downloads, and about a month after that, 2 million. That growth curve is well documented — but so is its driver: prominent YouTubers, including PewDiePie, Markiplier, Jacksepticeye, Etika, and the Game Grumps channel, published Let's Play videos that drove much of the traffic. The available sources document YouTube coverage as the spread mechanism; none of them attribute the spread to any itch.io platform feature — front-page placement, algorithmic surfacing, or otherwise. The game went on to win multiple IGN People's Choice Awards and the 2018 SXSW Gaming Awards' Matthew Crump Cultural Innovation Award, and later moved to Steam largely unchanged in form.

Buckshot Roulette shows a similar pattern in a more recent and more commercially explicit form. Developer Mike Klubnika released the game on itch.io on December 28, 2023, priced at "$2.99 or more." According to Wikipedia's account of its release history, it "quickly gained popularity on Twitch and TikTok, which subsequently led to increased popularity on YouTube" — again, external platforms, not itch.io's own discovery tools, are credited with the spread. Critical Reflex published it on Steam on April 4, 2024; it sold 1 million copies within two weeks of that Steam launch and 4 million by December 2024, with a multiplayer mode added that October. Checking the itch.io page directly (September 2026) confirms the version there still lacks the online leaderboard and multiplayer features the Steam release has gained, and the comment section shows visible, ongoing frustration from early itch.io buyers who feel their original purchase has been left behind by a version that no longer receives updates — a real and current pattern of sentiment, though not one this research can quantify precisely.

Pizza Tower followed a slower version of the same arc. Developer Tour de Pizza (designer "McPig," later joined by programmer "Sertif") posted playable demos on itch.io starting around 2018 and kept building in public for roughly five years, during which a Discord community grew from 5,000 to 20,000 members before being shut down. The game launched on Steam on January 26, 2023, published with Fangamer, and scored 89 on Metacritic — reported by secondary sources as the highest-rated 2D platformer on Steam. Here the "discovery" mechanism looks different again: not a single viral event, but years of sustained, self-organized community-building on Discord and social media around an itch.io demo, with itch.io functioning as the hosting layer rather than the thing actively surfacing the game to new audiences.

HoloCure – Save the Fans!, made by the solo developer Kay Yu, offers a fourth variant. Released free on itch.io on June 23, 2022, it drew 50,000 downloads on its first day alone — fast enough that itch.io's general-purpose discovery tools plausibly played a smaller role than the game's built-in audience: it is a roguelike shoot-'em-up built around VTubers from Hololive Production, a preexisting fandom with its own dense, high-traffic social media ecosystem that could plausibly explain rapid uptake independent of anything itch.io did. It later released on Steam (also free) on August 16, 2023, with explicit permission from Hololive's parent company Cover Corporation, drawing 45,000 concurrent players at release and 99% positive Steam reviews; the developer has stated he never intended to monetize the project.

Two cases in this set began life not as finished games at all, but as game-jam prototypes, which points to a genuinely different — and better-evidenced — discovery mechanism than itch.io's general browsing tools: jam infrastructure and jam-specific community rating. Celeste began as Celeste Classic, a PICO-8 platformer its own itch.io listing describes as "made in four days" by Maddy Thorson and Noel Berry (a secondary source, Wikipedia, gives a slightly different figure of three days, completed in August 2015 — a minor discrepancy this article notes rather than silently resolves). The full commercial game released January 25, 2018, scored in the high 80s to low 90s on Metacritic across platforms, won two D.I.C.E. Awards in 2019, and had sold 1.7 million copies by January 2025. Inscryption traces back even more explicitly to jam mechanics: Daniel Mullins has stated directly, in his own itch.io devlog, that he built the prototype Sacrifices Must Be Made for Ludum Dare 43, and that "the amount of interest and positivity directed at this little jam entry was a huge motivation boost for creating Inscryption," which Devolver Digital published on Steam on October 19, 2021. In both cases, the mechanism that first surfaced the work to an audience was a jam's own internal rating and visibility system — a different thing from itch.io's general-purpose browse, tag, or front-page tools, even though the jam entries themselves were hosted on itch.io.

One case remains mid-story and is worth including precisely because it has not resolved yet: Voices of the Void, a first-person sci-fi horror simulation by the developer "mrdrnose," remains itch.io-exclusive as of this research (checked at version 0.9.0-n), offered on a name-your-own-price basis, holding a 4.9-out-of-5 rating across 5,813 ratings — a rating profile that would look at home among the games discussed above. Its developer has stated a Steam release is planned but is not close, because the game is still being polished. This is presented here as a snapshot, not a prediction: the specific numbers will be outdated by the time anyone reads this, and there is no way to know from the available evidence whether it will follow the Doki Doki Literature Club/Buckshot Roulette pattern, the Pizza Tower pattern, or simply remain where it is indefinitely.

4. Case Studies: A Claim Classification

Table form makes the pattern across these seven cases easier to see than prose does. What is being classified here is not "is this game good" — a judgment this article does not attempt — but what kind of claim each part of its origin story represents.

Reading across the "stated cause" column, a pattern emerges that the article's central question requires stating directly: in every case with a documented cause, that cause is external to itch.io's own general-purpose discovery system — a YouTuber, a social platform, a jam's own rating mechanism, or a preexisting fan community. None of the seven cases in this record has a documented cause that traces back to itch.io's front-page curation, tags, or algorithmic modules as the thing that took the game from obscure to noticed. That is a meaningful finding about how itch.io's reputation as a hidden-gem factory actually operates, and it is different from the folk version of the claim, which tends to credit the platform itself as an active discovery engine.

5. Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations

The argument above has real limits, and the strongest objections to it deserve direct statement rather than a token mention.

Survivorship bias is the central problem, and it cuts against this article too. Seven cases, however well documented, are seven cases out of a hosted catalog that itch.io's own (imprecisely dated) figures put north of a million products. No source available to this research — not itch.io, not a third-party analytics firm — publishes any figure for what fraction of itch.io's total catalog receives more than a handful of downloads, reviews, or plays. It is entirely possible that itch.io hosts thousands of games as good as Celeste Classic or Sacrifices Must Be Made that never found their YouTuber, their jam spotlight, or their fandom, and simply remain unfound. This article's case studies can show what happened when discovery occurred; they cannot show how rare that outcome is relative to the platform's total volume, because no one — including itch.io — appears to measure that.

itch.io's own rating signal is not neutral evidence of "gem" quality either. A direct check of itch.io's "top-rated" browse page in September 2026 found that 11 of the top 14 listed games were visual novels, with high rating counts (multiple entries above 5,000–10,000 ratings each) — a genre concentration that may reflect the size and rating-participation habits of itch.io's visual-novel-reading community more than it reflects that genre's typical relationship to game quality broadly. This is a single-day snapshot, not a permanent finding, but it illustrates that even itch.io's own internal signal for "well-regarded" is shaped by which communities rate heavily on the platform, not by some genre-neutral measure of merit.

A competing framing of the whole argument is also worth stating directly: itch.io may not need to be an active discovery engine to be doing real work. This article has argued that itch.io functions as low-friction hosting and jam infrastructure rather than as the discovery mechanism itself — but low-friction, free, no-review hosting is arguably a precondition every one of these seven games needed before any external virality could happen at all. Sacrifices Must Be Made could not have won over a jam audience without somewhere free and immediate to host it; Doki Doki Literature Club's pay-what-you-want model, native to itch.io's business logic, is plausibly part of why YouTubers were willing to feature it at zero cost to their viewers. Crediting external virality as "the" cause while treating itch.io as merely incidental infrastructure may understate the platform's role as a necessary, if not sufficient, condition.

Finally, the mature-content deindexing episode from July 2025 complicates any clean story about itch.io as a stable engine of discovery of any kind, gem-producing or otherwise: a platform whose entire mature-tagged catalog can be made briefly unfindable by a single payment-processor dispute is not a fully reliable long-term archive, whatever its day-to-day discovery mechanics look like.

6. Limitations

This article relies substantially on Wikipedia's aggregation of press coverage for several of its case studies (Doki Doki Literature Club, Pizza Tower, Buckshot Roulette, HoloCure, Celeste), rather than independently re-tracing every fact to its original press report. Wikipedia's entries on commercially released, widely covered games are generally well-sourced and cross-checked by many editors, which is why this article treats them as Tier 2 evidence rather than declining to use them — but it is a different evidentiary standard than the developer-primary-source material used for the Inscryption and Celeste Classic jam-origin claims, and readers should weigh it accordingly.

Several figures in this piece are explicitly time-stamped snapshots rather than stable facts: the itch.io product-count figure, the Voices of the Void rating count and development status, and the top-rated-page genre breakdown will all have changed, possibly substantially, by the time this article is read. This is stated wherever it applies rather than presented as settled.

This article also could not verify itch.io's current (2026) discovery algorithm in the same detail as its 2019 documented baseline, and it could not verify the precise dollar figures or independent sourcing for itch.io's smaller charitable bundles (Section 2) to the same standard as the Bundle for Racial Justice and Equality figure. Both gaps are stated rather than filled with an estimate.

Most importantly: this article does not, and cannot, answer whether itch.io hosts a disproportionately high rate of high-quality, undiscovered games relative to other free-hosting platforms, relative to Steam's own \$100 tier of unknown releases, or relative to any other baseline. No comparative study of that kind exists in the available evidence. Every claim in this piece about specific games that did break out should be read as exactly that — an account of specific, documented cases — and not as evidence about the base rate of hidden quality on the platform as a whole.

7. Conclusion

What we know: itch.io is a very large, minimally curated hosting platform — on the order of a million-plus listed products by one imprecisely sourced 2024 figure, up from a documented 200,000 in 2019 — that by its own 2019 account relies on human curation, tagging, and periodic bundles rather than a primary recommendation algorithm. Several specific, well-documented games (Celeste, Inscryption, Doki Doki Literature Club, Pizza Tower, Buckshot Roulette, HoloCure) did travel from itch.io obscurity to substantial commercial and critical recognition, and in every one of these cases the specific mechanism credited with that spread — a jam's internal rating system, a YouTuber's Let's Play, a preexisting fandom, or years of self-organized community building — was external to itch.io's own general-purpose discovery tools.

What we think: The evidence is consistent with a model in which itch.io functions primarily as low-friction, permissionless infrastructure — a place a game can exist for free or near-free the moment it's ready — rather than as an active discovery engine that finds good games and puts them in front of the right audience. Under that model, itch.io's real contribution to the "hidden gem" pipeline is less about surfacing quality and more about removing the barriers (cost, review, gatekeeping) that would otherwise prevent a jam prototype or a fan project from existing in a findable state at all, leaving the actual finding to jam communities, streamers, YouTubers, and social platforms itch.io does not control.

What we don't know: No source — including itch.io itself — publishes data on what fraction of its catalog is ever meaningfully discovered, reviewed, or played, which means this article cannot state, and no one currently can state with evidence, how representative these seven success stories are of the platform's total contents. It is equally possible that itch.io is unusually good at incubating undiscovered quality, or that it hosts an enormous quantity of unremarkable and unremarked-upon work alongside a small number of famous outliers, in roughly the proportion any sufficiently large, unmoderated hosting platform would produce. Nothing in the available record settles that question, and readers encountering a confident claim that itch.io is "full of hidden gems" — including, implicitly, the framing of this article's own title — should recognize that claim as an appealing story built on real but limited evidence, not a measured fact.
