# Deconstructing Digital Folklore

## A Claim-by-Claim Investigation of Five Iconic Video Game Myths

*Game Research Writer | August 2026*

# Introduction

When five of video gaming's most durable urban legends are checked directly against primary interviews, technical documentation, and archival source-code leaks, which parts of the popular story survive contact with the evidence, and which parts turn out to be embellishments added somewhere along the chain of retelling?

This piece examines five cases spanning three decades of arcade and console gaming: the "Sheng Long" hoax in Street Fighter II (1991-1992), the "Mew under the truck" rumor in Pokémon Red and Blue (1996), the "L is Real 2401" plaque mystery in Super Mario 64 (1996), the "Nude Raider" patch controversy around Tomb Raider (1996-1999), and the broader, still-contested claim that violent video games cause real-world criminal violence. The first four are self-contained legends with identifiable origins, named participants, and largely documented resolutions. The fifth case is different in kind: it is not a rumor about a specific game but an ongoing empirical dispute conducted in peer-reviewed literature and institutional policy statements. It is included because it illustrates the same underlying pattern — an information gap filled first by inference and later, unevenly, by evidence — operating at a much larger scale.

Where the available sources support a claim, this piece says so and names the source. Where a popular retelling overstates what the record actually shows — a causal claim with no confirming statement, a joke misattributed to the wrong sequence of events, a numerical coincidence with no documented significance — that gap is flagged rather than smoothed over. Several claims that circulate in fan wikis and video essays about these five legends did not survive this verification process; they are noted as removed or downgraded, along with the reason.

# Context: Why Gaming Myths Formed

Before broadband internet, ROM disassembly tools, and community datamining were common, players had four main channels for learning about a game's hidden content: word of mouth, playground conversation, print magazines, and early dial-up forums. None of these channels offered a way to independently verify a claim against a game's underlying code. A rumor's credibility depended less on evidence than on the perceived authority of whoever repeated it — a friend who claimed to have done it, or a magazine that printed it as a "trick."

Three technical conditions recur across the cases examined here. First, localization introduced errors that were invisible to the original Japanese development teams but became load-bearing for English-speaking players' theories (Sheng Long). Second, storage and memory constraints on 1990s cartridges and consoles produced both real cut content and, separately, compressed or reused visual assets that were never meant to convey information at all (Mew's insertion into freed cartridge space; the reused "L is Real" plaque texture). Third, marketing strategies that emphasized mystery, sex appeal, or hidden mechanics gave commercial incentive to publishers and, in one documented case, a magazine editorial staff, to seed or amplify a rumor rather than correct it (the EGM Sheng Long hoax; Eidos's Tomb Raider marketing). These conditions are not evidence that any specific myth was manufactured; they describe the environment in which myths of very different origins — translation errors, developer pranks, journalistic hoaxes, and pareidolia — could all spread through the same social channels and, in several cases, come back around to influence the games themselves.

# Methodology and Source Hierarchy

Claims below are graded on a four-tier hierarchy. Tier 1 is primary evidence: developer interviews, official documentation, leaked source code, and direct technical inspection. Tier 2 is strong secondary evidence: journalism and technical publications with documented methodology (Wikipedia articles are treated here only as pointers to their own cited primary/secondary sources, not as authorities in themselves). Tier 3 is community evidence: fan wikis, reverse-engineering write-ups, and forum research that is plausible but not independently corroborated. Tier 4 is weak evidence: unsourced forum or social-media claims. Tier 3 and Tier 4 material is flagged as such wherever it appears and is never presented as confirmed fact. A full claim-by-claim ledger with confidence ratings appears as an appendix.

# Case Study 1: Sheng Long and the Street Fighter II Mistranslation

## What Happened

Ryu's original Japanese victory quote in Street Fighter II (1991) reads: 昇龍拳を破らぬ限り、お前に勝ち目はない — "If you cannot overcome the Shoryuken [Rising Dragon Fist], you have no chance of winning." Shoryuken (昇龍拳) is the name of Ryu's uppercut special move. The kanji for "rising dragon" (昇龍) can also be read using Chinese pinyin as Shēng Lóng. Capcom's English localization for the arcade release rendered the line as "You must defeat Sheng Long to stand a chance," turning the name of an attack into what read like the name of a person. Home console versions, including the Super Nintendo release, corrected the line to reference the "Dragon Punch," but the SNES instruction manual's character biographies compounded the error by describing a "Master Sheng Long" as Ryu and Ken's teacher, giving the phantom character an apparent canonical foothold.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed. This sequence is independently corroborated by Capcom's own localized manual text, contemporaneous screenshots of the arcade dialogue, and multiple gaming-history retrospectives.

## The Electronic Gaming Monthly Hoax

In its April 1992 issue, Electronic Gaming Monthly published a fabricated "Tips and Tricks" walkthrough claiming players could fight Sheng Long as a secret boss. The claimed method: play as Ryu, take no damage through the entire game, then force ten consecutive draw rounds against the final boss, M. Bison, without landing or taking a hit. Sheng Long would then supposedly appear, throw Bison off screen, and fight the player with an amplified moveset. According to later published accounts from people involved, EGM editor Ken Williams — who wrote under the pseudonym "Sushi-X" — conceived the hoax and used early Adobe Photoshop software to fabricate supporting screenshots; colleagues Ray Price and Martin Alessi contributed ideas, and founding editor Steve Harris approved running it. The magazine repeated the joke in April 1997 for Street Fighter III, publishing doctored images of an aged Sheng Long throwing one-handed fireballs.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the hoax's existence and printed content (contemporaneous magazine issue, widely archived). Strong inference for the internal authorship account (Williams/Price/Alessi/Harris), which rests on retrospective interviews rather than contemporaneous internal records — plausible and uncontradicted, but not independently documented at the time.

## Design Analysis: From Hoax to Canon

Capcom introduced Akuma (Gouki in Japan) as a genuinely hidden secret boss in Super Street Fighter II Turbo (1994), reachable through a real, if less elaborate, set of hidden-boss conditions that echoed the structure of EGM's fictional Sheng Long encounter. It is widely believed among fans and journalists that the Sheng Long hoax directly inspired Akuma's design. Capcom has never publicly confirmed or denied this connection. This is a case where the causality test matters: a secret, unlockable "super boss" that interrupts the final fight is also a recognizable genre convention independent of any single rumor, and Capcom could have arrived at Akuma through ordinary design instincts of the era. What can be said with more confidence is that the myth's popularity and Akuma's arrival were closely correlated in time, and Capcom's later, explicit references to Sheng Long (an Xbox 360 "Sheng Long is in Another Castle" achievement for defeating Akuma, and 25th-anniversary hoax-commemoration artwork in 2017) show that the company came to treat the hoax as part of its own promotional history, whatever the truth of Akuma's origin.

Separately, the idea that Capcom "incorporated Sheng Long into the canon" by way of the character Gouken deserves a correction: Gouken did not originate from the EGM hoax. He first appeared as Ryu and Ken's master in Masaomi Kanzaki's 1993 Street Fighter II manga, a year after the hoax, and was folded into game canon later. Street Fighter IV producer Yoshinori Ono has said Gouken's addition to the games partly responded to lingering fan interest in the Sheng Long story, which is a real and documented feedback effect, but it is a case of an existing character being connected to the myth's fan appeal, not the myth generating the character from nothing. Sheng Long himself finally appears as a fightable non-player character in Street Fighter 6's "World Tour" mode, using artwork Capcom commissioned for the hoax's 25th anniversary.

CONFIDENCE: The Akuma-hoax connection is a strong inference supported by circumstantial and promotional evidence, but explicitly unconfirmed by Capcom — treated here as "widely believed to have inspired," not "caused." The Gouken originating in EGM's hoax claim is unsupported and has been removed from this account; the correct, confirmed lineage is the 1993 manga.

## Limitations and Unresolved Questions

No Capcom developer has gone on record explaining Akuma's design origin in detail, so the strength of the EGM-to-Akuma link rests on inference from timing and later corporate winking references, not a developer statement. The precise wording of Ken Williams's role also comes from retrospective accounts rather than 1992-era documentation.

# Case Study 2: Mew and the S.S. Anne Truck

## What Happened

Pokémon Red and Green (Japan, 1996) shipped with a 151st, unlisted Pokémon: Mew. Programmer Shigeki Morimoto has stated in later interviews that he added Mew near the end of development, after debug tools were stripped out freed up a small amount of remaining cartridge space, and that he did this without formal authorization — reportedly after being told not to make further changes to the game. Multiple technology and gaming outlets report the addition took roughly 300 bytes, though this specific figure comes from secondary reporting rather than a directly quoted developer statement citing that number.

Separately, a genuine software bug — commonly called the "Mew glitch" or, in some accounts, tied to the "Trainer Fly" exploit — allowed players to manipulate wild-encounter memory in ways that could trigger an unofficial wild Mew encounter. This glitch is real and technically documented by the ROM-hacking and speedrunning communities (Tier 3 evidence, but consistent across many independent write-ups). Its existence gave players who encountered Mew unofficially a plausible, if false, causal story to build on: since Mew appeared unexpectedly during play, many concluded there must be a legitimate, undocumented way to find it in the game world, rather than a memory-manipulation exploit.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed that Morimoto added Mew secretly and without authorization late in development. Moderate/strong inference on the specific "300 bytes" figure, which appears consistently across secondary sources but is not traced here to a primary quote using that exact number. Confirmed (via consistent Tier 2/3 technical documentation) that a real glitch could produce unofficial Mew encounters.

## The Truck Myth Mechanics

A small, non-interactive pickup truck sits on a dock beside the S.S. Anne in Vermilion City. Under normal play, the ship departs permanently once the player collects HM01 (Cut) aboard it. Players discovered that deliberately losing a battle immediately after obtaining Cut sends the player back to a Pokémon Center while the ship remains docked, and that returning later with HM03 (Surf) lets them approach the truck again. Because the truck was a visually unique asset with no other narrative function, a rumor spread — its exact point of origin is not established in the available record — that using HM04 (Strength) on the truck would move it and reveal Mew, or a hidden Team Rocket facility. In fact, the truck sprite carries no interaction script, no collision behavior tied to Strength, and no event flags of any kind.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed that the truck has no interaction script (consistent across ROM data documentation). Explicitly unconfirmed who originated the "truck hides Mew" claim specifically, as opposed to the broader, well-documented rumor that Mew existed somewhere unofficial in the game. Available sources describe the truck rumor's existence and spread but do not establish who started it or precisely when it attached itself to that specific sprite; this piece does not speculate further.

## Design Analysis: Corporate Response

Nintendo and Game Freak used the broader "secret 151st Pokémon" mystery as a genuine marketing asset, running a CoroCoro Comic distribution promotion in spring 1996 that offered official Mew transfers to a limited number of winners — reportedly around 20 winners from roughly 78,000 entries, far exceeding Nintendo's internal expectations. In the 2004 Game Boy Advance remakes, Pokémon FireRed and LeafGreen, Game Freak placed a "Lava Cookie" item underneath the S.S. Anne truck — an explicit, documented homage to the rumor, described in developer-adjacent commentary as a nod to the fans who believed in it.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the CoroCoro promotion and its scale. Confirmed for the FireRed/LeafGreen item placement as an intentional reference to the myth.

## Limitations and Unresolved Questions

The available sources document that the truck rumor existed and was false, and that it was later honored in a remake. They do not establish who first proposed the truck specifically as Mew's hiding place, nor exactly when that detail entered circulation relative to the broader "secret Pokémon" rumor. This piece does not fill that gap with speculation.

# Case Study 3: "L is Real 2401" and Luigi in Super Mario 64

## What Happened

A small bronze-colored plaque sits on a star-shaped statue in the courtyard of Princess Peach's castle in Super Mario 64 (1996). At the Nintendo 64's rendering resolution and texture compression, the inscription was illegible to the naked eye during normal play. Community image analysis conducted years later — using enhanced imaging on original hardware — determined the text actually reads "A Secret Star lies here," not "L is Real 2401." The "L is Real 2401" and "Eternal Star" readings were both plausible-sounding misreadings of the same blurry, low-resolution texture, and the plaque asset itself is reused elsewhere: the same texture appears in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, unrelated to Luigi.

Around this misread text, players built theories that collecting all 120 stars, gathering a theoretical maximum of 2,672 coins, or running around the courtyard fountain exactly 2,401 times would unlock a playable Luigi. None of these actions unlock anything in the released game; no interaction tied to the plaque or the fountain exists in the shipped code.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the corrected plaque transcription via direct hardware/image analysis. Confirmed that the same texture asset recurs in Ocarina of Time. Confirmed that no unlock mechanism tied to the plaque, fountain, or coin count exists in the released game (absence is well documented by decades of unsuccessful attempts and, more directly, by inspection of the game's code by the reverse-engineering community).

## Developer Statements: A Real, Separate Truth

While the plaque was meaningless, Luigi genuinely was built into pre-release versions of Super Mario 64. In a translated 1996-era interview compiled by shmuplations.com, Shigeru Miyamoto stated: "Well… until February, he was in the game. (laughs) Ultimately, due to memory issues, we had to take him out. Then we were going to include him in a Mario Bros.-style minigame, but because most users probably only have that one controller when they first buy their N64, for that reason (and others) we decided not to." Miyamoto separately described an early two-player prototype in which the screen dynamically resized based on how far apart the two characters moved, which the team abandoned citing processing-speed constraints on the hardware — though one write-up covering this remark notes some ambiguity about whether Miyamoto was describing Super Mario 64 itself or a related, unreleased "Mario 64 II" prototype.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed, Tier 1. This is a direct, on-the-record developer statement giving both a removal date (February 1996, ahead of the June 1996 Japanese launch) and two stated reasons: cartridge/system memory limits, and a practical concern about single-controller ownership among early N64 buyers. The two-player-screen-resizing anecdote is confirmed as a real prototype but its exact source game (Super Mario 64 vs. a follow-up prototype) is not fully disambiguated in available reporting.

## The 2020 "Gigaleak" Validation

On July 25, 2020, a large unauthorized leak of internal Nintendo source material and assets — commonly called the "Gigaleak" — included archived development files for Super Mario 64. These files contained Luigi-specific character textures and, according to contemporaneous reporting, other assets consistent with an early playable Luigi. Community developers subsequently used these leaked files to reconstruct a working Luigi character inside the Super Mario 64 engine. Available reporting on the leak documents the existence of Luigi-specific assets but does not, on its own, provide a detailed technical breakdown (full character model vs. partial texture set, extent of functional code) beyond what the Miyamoto interview already established about the character's development status and removal.

A claim occasionally attached to this story — that the July 25, 2020 leak date falls exactly "24 years and one month" after the game's June 23, 1996 Japanese release — is arithmetically approximate at best (the actual gap is 24 years and roughly one month and two days) and is not treated as significant in any source consulted for this piece. It reads as a numerological flourish rather than a documented fact, and it has been removed from this account.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed that the Gigaleak included Luigi-specific Super Mario 64 assets. Speculation, unsupported: the "24 years, 1 month" date coincidence is not documented as meaningful anywhere in the sourced material and has been dropped.

## Limitations and Unresolved Questions

The 1998-era claim that a Nintendo "Game Play Counselor" told a fan by letter that the plaque was "simply a joke" circulates in fan communities (reported as originating from a Reddit post of a letter) but is Tier 3/4 evidence — a single unverified secondhand letter, not an official statement. It is mentioned here only as context for how the mystery was informally addressed long before the 2020 leak, not as confirmation of anything beyond what the corrected image transcription already establishes.

# Case Study 4: The "Nude Raider" Patch

## What Happened

Following Tomb Raider's 1996 release, a rumor spread that a hidden input sequence would remove protagonist Lara Croft's clothing. Eidos Interactive's marketing for the character leaned heavily on her sex appeal in mainstream and men's-interest media, which plausibly primed audiences to expect this kind of "adult" hidden content, though no source consulted here documents Eidos or Core Design deliberately planting the specific nude-code rumor. Co-creator Paul Douglas has stated that Core Design's management floated the idea of an official nude mode "perhaps half-jokingly" during development and that the development team refused: "there certainly wasn't anything in the game that did that." No native nude code or hidden asset shipped in any official console or PC release of the base game.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed, on developer record, that no official nude code exists in the base game and that management raised and the team rejected the idea internally.

## The Third-Party Patch and Legal Response

Because no code existed, an unidentified fan created an unauthorized PC modification, commonly called "Nude Raider," that replaced Lara Croft's texture and mesh files with a nude model. This is documented as a hacked asset swap, not a discovered in-game feature. Eidos responded with cease-and-desist notices to sites hosting the patch and took possession of the domain name nuderaider.com. In Tomb Raider II (1997), programmer Gavin Rummery has stated that the team added a joke "shower scene" at the end of the game, in which Lara shoots the player character for spying on her — his stated intent was to mock players still chasing the nonexistent nude code, not to reward them. A widely repeated claim that Core Design instead made Lara's character model "explode" in response to entering the rumored cheat code was not corroborated by any source consulted for this piece; the developer-confirmed joke is the shower/shooting scene, and the "exploding Lara" version appears to be a conflation or embellishment that should not be treated as fact.

Separately, Eidos and Core Design ran a tongue-in-cheek Tomb Raider III advertisement in 1998 stating "we improved everything you asked for, but sorry, still no nude code" — a rare instance of the publisher directly, publicly acknowledging the myth in its own marketing.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the patch's existence and Eidos's cease-and-desist/domain-seizure response (corroborated across a Wikipedia summary citing a contemporaneous BBC report and a dedicated Tomb Raider fan-wiki account). Confirmed, Tier 1, for the TR2 shower-scene joke via direct developer statement. Unsupported and removed: the claim that the joke code caused Lara to "explode."

## Model Contracts and Brand Control

Model Nell McAndrew served as the official promotional face of Lara Croft from roughly 1998 to 1999. After she posed for Playboy in 1999, Eidos ended her promotional role. The available record indicates the proximate legal trigger was not the nude photography itself but a trademark dispute: Playboy's cover referenced Lara Croft and Tomb Raider by name, and Eidos threatened legal action over the unauthorized use of its intellectual property, which led to those references being removed from the cover and to the end of McAndrew's contract. This is a meaningfully different causal story than "Eidos fired her for posing nude" — the documented trigger is brand/trademark enforcement, with the Playboy appearance as the occasion rather than the sole stated cause.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the timeline and outcome. Strong inference, not certainty, on the precise weighting of "trademark enforcement" versus "reputational concern about nudity" in Eidos's actual internal decision, since no internal Eidos statement enumerating its reasoning was located.

## Limitations and Unresolved Questions

No source consulted identifies the original creator of the Nude Raider patch by name, or pins down an exact release date beyond "sometime after the 1996 release, discussed publicly by 1997." The precise commercial calculation behind Eidos's marketing strategy — how much it depended on, versus merely tolerated, the nude-code rumor — is not addressed in any primary developer or executive statement located for this piece.

# Case Study 5: Video Games and Real-World Violence

## What Happened

Unlike the first four cases, this is not a myth about a specific game's hidden content but a decades-long public claim that exposure to violent video games causes real-world criminal violence, including mass shootings. The American Psychological Association convened task forces to review the empirical literature, publishing a report in August 2015 and a revised resolution in February 2020.

The APA's 2015 statement, publicized through comprehensive review of more than 150 studies, concluded there was a consistent, small-to-moderate association between violent video game exposure and increased aggressive behavior, aggressive cognition, and aggressive affect, alongside decreased empathy and prosocial behavior in the period immediately following play. Task force chair Mark Appelbaum stated explicitly that "there is very limited research addressing whether violent video games cause people to commit acts of criminal violence," and the task force described the evidence linking games to criminal violence or delinquency as insufficient to support such a claim. The February 2020 resolution reaffirmed this distinction and again cautioned against attributing mass shootings or other criminal violence to video game exposure, framing violent crime as a multifactorial outcome involving personal violence history, psychiatric illness, substance use, and socioeconomic conditions, among other risk factors.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the existence, dates, and general conclusions of both the 2015 report and 2020 resolution, and for the Appelbaum quotation, based on APA press communications reported by multiple independent outlets. This piece was not able to directly retrieve and quote the full primary-source PDF text of the APA task force report due to an access restriction on apa.org encountered during research; the conclusions above are corroborated across multiple independent secondary reports of that primary document rather than drawn from the PDF itself, and are flagged accordingly.

## Independent Reanalysis and Effect-Size Dispute

A 2020 reanalysis by Christopher Ferguson, Allen Copenhaver, and Patrick Markey, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, revisited the 18 studies underlying the APA's 2015 aggressive-behavior conclusion. The reanalysis reported the original aggregate effect size as approximately r = .18–.19 as characterized by the APA, versus a recalculated r ≈ .09 using the same studies, and found that effect sizes were systematically higher in studies with acknowledged methodological weaknesses (non-best-practice designs, citation bias) than in studies without them. Restricting the analysis to what the authors judged the more methodologically rigorous "best practices" studies produced an effect close to zero (r ≈ −.025), and the authors argued that, expressed as variance explained, the true relationship might account for well under one percent of variation in aggressive behavior. They concluded this evidence base was too weak to support the APA's 2015 resolution as written and recommended it be retired.

A separate, earlier flashpoint is a 2013 open letter signed by roughly 230 media-effects scholars, criticizing the APA's general stance on violent media as "misleading and alarmist" — documented evidence that the scientific community itself is not unanimous on how to weigh this literature, independent of any single study's findings.

CONFIDENCE: Confirmed for the existence, authorship, and headline figures of the Ferguson et al. reanalysis (peer-reviewed, indexed on PubMed). Confirmed for the existence of the 2013 open letter as a documented instance of scholarly dissent, though this piece did not independently verify the exact signatory count beyond secondary reporting.

## Design and Policy Analysis

The video game industry's response to this dispute has been institutional rather than creative: the Entertainment Software Rating Board's content-rating system, and platform-level parental controls, are commonly cited as downstream policy responses to public and legislative concern about violent content, though this piece did not trace a documented, direct causal chain from the APA's specific findings to ESRB rating criteria. That connection is treated here as plausible context, not a confirmed causal claim.

CONFIDENCE: Weak inference. The ESRB predates the APA's 2015/2020 reports by two decades (established 1994), so it cannot have been created in response to them; any influence would have to run through earlier iterations of the same underlying public debate, which this piece did not separately verify.

## Limitations and Unresolved Questions

This case involves an active scientific and methodological dispute rather than a settled historical record. The APA and Ferguson-led reanalysis camps disagree not just on interpretation but on which underlying studies should count as valid evidence at all. Readers should treat both the APA's small-effect conclusion and Ferguson's near-zero reanalysis as competing, professionally credentialed positions rather than a debate with a clear winner. What is well supported across both camps is the narrower, negative claim: no study reviewed by either side establishes a causal link between violent video game exposure and criminal violence.

# Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations

The strongest general objection to this piece's framework is that "developer feedback loop" narratives — the idea that Capcom's Akuma or Nintendo's FireRed/LeafGreen truck item were direct responses to specific fan myths — may overstate intentionality that publishers have never confirmed. Secret superbosses, hidden characters, and knowing easter-egg references to fan speculation are common design and marketing practices across the industry generally, independent of any single myth (later Street Fighter and Tekken games, for instance, have repeatedly added secret bosses without a documented hoax behind each one). Attributing Akuma specifically to the Sheng Long hoax, absent a Capcom statement, risks the same interpretive error the causality test is meant to catch: mistaking an industry-wide convention for a bespoke response to one rumor. This piece has tried to flag that uncertainty in Case Study 1 rather than assert the connection as fact, but the underlying ambiguity is worth restating here directly.

A second objection applies to the violence-causality case specifically: the APA's own task forces have been criticized, including by the 2013 open letter of media-effects scholars, as insufficiently independent from advocacy positions that predate the research review. If the committees convened to evaluate this literature were not neutral, their conclusions — on either side, including Ferguson's own more skeptical reanalyses — may reflect selection and framing choices as much as the underlying data. This piece has presented both the APA and Ferguson positions rather than adjudicate between them, but a reader should understand that "the science is unsettled" is itself a contested characterization; some psychologists would argue the APA's small-effect finding is adequately supported and that Ferguson's null result reflects an overly strict "best practices" filter that discards legitimate studies, not a more accurate one.

A third, narrower objection concerns the two truck/plaque-style stories (Mew and "L is Real 2401"). Both cases show that a specific misreading of an ambiguous visual asset (a static truck sprite; a low-resolution plaque) does not, by itself, prove where or how the underlying rumor about hidden content originated. This piece was able to confirm what the assets do not do (no truck interaction script; no plaque-triggered unlock), but could not establish, from the sources available, the first point at which either myth attached itself to that specific asset rather than some other unexplained detail in the game world. A skeptical reader is right to note that "the truck/plaque is a red herring" and "we don't know who started the specific truck/plaque theory" are two separate claims, and only the first is fully supported here.

# Limitations

This investigation relied primarily on secondary reporting (Tier 2) corroborated across multiple independent outlets, supplemented by a smaller number of direct primary-source quotations (Tier 1: the Miyamoto shmuplations interview, the Paul Douglas and Gavin Rummery developer statements on Tomb Raider, Capcom's own SNES manual text, and press-reported APA statements). Community wikis and fan-research sites (Tier 3) were used only to corroborate points already supported by stronger sources, or flagged explicitly where they were the best available evidence (for example, some details of Mew's exact byte count and the truck rumor's unknown originator). No claim in the case studies above rests solely on Tier 4 evidence.

Two access limitations affected this piece directly. The APA's own report and resolution PDFs (apa.org) returned an automated security-service response during fetch attempts and could not be quoted directly; conclusions attributed to the APA are instead drawn from multiple independent secondary reports describing those documents' content, which is a weaker form of sourcing than quoting the primary text and is flagged wherever it applies. Several fan-community claims — the originator of the Mew-truck rumor, the exact "300 bytes" figure for Mew's insertion, and a purported 1998 Nintendo customer-support letter about the Mario 64 plaque — could not be traced to verifiable primary documentation and are presented with corresponding lower confidence or omitted from the confirmed record.

# Conclusion

## What We Know

Sheng Long began as a documented translation error, compounded by an English-language manual, and was then deliberately mythologized by Electronic Gaming Monthly as an April Fools' joke with named (retrospectively self-reported) authors. Mew was secretly, undocumented-ly added to Pokémon Red/Green by Shigeki Morimoto using freed cartridge space, and a real software glitch let players trigger unofficial encounters, but the specific S.S. Anne truck sits inert in the game's code. The Super Mario 64 plaque reads "A Secret Star lies here," not "L is Real 2401," and is a reused generic texture — while, separately and confirmed by Miyamoto's own words, a real playable Luigi existed in development until February 1996 and was cut for stated memory and controller-availability reasons. No native "nude code" ever existed in Tomb Raider; the "Nude Raider" patch was an unauthorized third-party PC modification, and Core Design's documented in-joke response was a shower scene where Lara shoots the player, not an exploding character model. The APA's task forces have twice concluded that violent video games show a small statistical association with aggressive behavior but insufficient evidence to link them to criminal violence.

## What We Think

Akuma's design in Super Street Fighter II Turbo was very likely shaped, at least in promotional and cultural terms, by the popularity of the Sheng Long hoax, even though Capcom has never confirmed this and the underlying "secret superboss" design pattern is common enough industry-wide that the connection cannot be treated as proven. Eidos's decision to end Nell McAndrew's promotional contract was probably driven primarily by trademark protection rather than disapproval of nudity per se, based on the documented cover dispute, though no internal Eidos statement settles the relative weight of those motives. The magnitude of violent video games' real effect on aggression is most plausibly somewhere between the APA's 2015 estimate and Ferguson's 2020 reanalysis — small in either case, and clearly insufficient, under either camp's own findings, to explain criminal violence.

## What We Don't Know

No source located for this piece identifies who first proposed that the S.S. Anne truck specifically hid Mew, or precisely when that detail entered circulation. No source establishes definitively whether Miyamoto's remarks about a resizable-screen two-player prototype describe Super Mario 64 itself or a related, unreleased follow-up project. No primary Eidos or Core Design statement enumerates the exact commercial calculus behind Tomb Raider's marketing relative to the nude-code rumor, or names the original creator of the Nude Raider patch. And the underlying scientific dispute over violent media's real-world effects on aggression remains genuinely unresolved between credentialed researchers using the same underlying studies — a case where the correct conclusion, at least for now, is not a synthesis but an accurate description of the disagreement itself.
