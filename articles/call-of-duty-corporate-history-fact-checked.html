The Corporate Architecture of Call of Duty: Studio Fractures, Engine Lineage, and the Economics of an Annual Franchise

A Game Research Writer analysis. Compiled and fact-checked August 2026.

1. Introduction

Call of Duty is one of the few interactive entertainment properties whose corporate history is as consequential as its design history. The franchise has shipped nearly every year since 2003, survived the departure of its founding studio's entire leadership, absorbed at least four distinct proprietary game engines into one shared codebase, and became the central regulatory exhibit in the largest acquisition in the history of the games industry. Understanding why the series looks the way it does in 2026 — an annually rotating three-studio production line, a unified engine, a free-to-play battle royale wrapper, and a subscription-first release strategy under Microsoft — requires separating the well-documented record from the version of the story that has calcified into franchise folklore.

This analysis traces that record from 2015, Inc.'s Tulsa, Oklahoma origins through Microsoft's 2023 acquisition of Activision Blizzard, and flags the points where the popular narrative outruns the evidence. Confidence levels are stated explicitly throughout: some of this history is confirmed by contemporaneous filings and court records; some rests on a single retrospective interview; and at least one commonly repeated figure — the claimed $75.4 billion final cost of the Microsoft deal — could not be corroborated against any primary or secondary source reviewed and has been removed rather than repeated.

2. Context: From Medal of Honor to a Rejected James Bond Pitch (1995–2002)

Call of Duty's institutional lineage begins one company removed from Activision. DreamWorks Interactive was founded in March 1995 as a joint venture between DreamWorks SKG and Microsoft, with day-to-day leadership under Alan Hartman, Glenn Entis, and John Skeel. Steven Spielberg is widely credited in popular accounts as the studio's founder; the more precise record is that he negotiated the Microsoft financing that made the venture possible but did not hold a formal founder role in its corporate structure. This distinction matters for accuracy even though it doesn't change the outcome: DreamWorks Interactive shipped Medal of Honor in 1999, a war shooter that established the template Call of Duty would later compete against directly.

Electronic Arts acquired the studio on February 24, 2000, for a reported $10 million, renaming it EA Los Angeles and retaining the Medal of Honor IP. The team — including Jason West and Vince Zampella, who had by then moved to 2015, Inc., a Tulsa, Oklahoma studio founded in 1997 by Tom Kudirka — went on to build Medal of Honor: Allied Assault (2002), which sold roughly 1.5 million copies in its first year and was well reviewed for its Normandy-landing set piece.

What happened next is the subject of one of the franchise's most repeated origin stories, and it is worth stating precisely how well-sourced it is. According to an account Michael Condrey — then an EA developer on the James Bond games, later a co-founder of Sledgehammer Games — gave in a Call of Duty retrospective documentary, West and Zampella pitched EA directly to develop the PC port of James Bond 007: Nightfire around 2001–2002. EA passed and awarded the contract to Gearbox Software instead. This is a single-source, retrospective account rather than a contemporaneously documented business decision; it is credible because it comes from a named, direct participant in the surrounding events, but it should be read as testimony, not as a filed record. What is independently confirmed is the outcome: with no long-term EA contract secured, roughly 20 of 2015, Inc.'s 27 employees resigned, and West and Zampella founded Infinity Ward in May 2002 with a founding roster that ultimately included 22 former 2015 staffers.

Activision moved quickly. It provided $1.5 million for a 30% stake in the new studio to fund development of a war shooter, and — the day after the original Call of Duty's PC release on October 29, 2003, not before it, as is sometimes claimed — exercised its option to acquire the remaining 70%, making Infinity Ward a wholly owned subsidiary. Call of Duty (2003) shipped with dynamic squad AI, multi-perspective Allied campaigns, and sensory distortion effects during explosions, establishing a structural template EA's own Medal of Honor series would spend the rest of the decade responding to.

3. Evidence: The 2010 Leadership Crisis and the Origin of the Tri-Studio Model

Call of Duty's console expansion was uneven at first. Spark Unlimited's Call of Duty: Finest Hour (2004) brought the series to consoles but ended in litigation between Spark and Activision over the terms of that contract. Infinity Ward returned for Call of Duty 2 (2005), a flagship Xbox 360 launch title built on an internally upgraded engine (IW 2.0) with DirectX 9 support. To hold the annual release cadence, Activision brought in Treyarch — which had been running its own id Tech 3-derived codebase, sometimes referred to as Treyarch NGL — to build Call of Duty 3 (2006) on a compressed schedule. The specific claim that this schedule was "eight months" could not be verified against any source reviewed and is not repeated here; what is well documented is that Treyarch was assigned the project on short notice to preserve the yearly release rhythm, and that Call of Duty 3 was the first entry with expanded console multiplayer and vehicle combat.

Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare (2007) is the inflection point most industry historians point to, and the evidence supports that reading. Infinity Ward's shift to a contemporary setting, combined with a persistent multiplayer progression system — experience points, unlockable perks, weapon attachments, a create-a-class loadout system — reshaped first-person shooter design broadly, not just within the franchise. Its 2009 sequel, Modern Warfare 2, extended that commercial success and, according to multiple contemporaneous and retrospective accounts, also triggered the crisis that reshaped the company.

West and Zampella were terminated by Activision on March 1, 2010. Activision's stated reason was insubordination and breach of contract; West and Zampella maintained they were fired in retaliation for attempting to renegotiate their contracts for greater creative and financial independence. Reporting at the time cited approximately $36 million in withheld bonuses and royalties as the initial point of dispute — a figure that should be read as the early 2010 estimate, not the final legal claim; the subsequent lawsuit's damages figures grew substantially larger as litigation proceeded, before the case was settled out of court. In the months that followed, "several dozen" Infinity Ward employees resigned, according to available secondary sourcing — a real and significant exodus, though the exact headcount (sometimes rounded up to "nearly 40" in popular retellings) and the extent to which departures were motivated by solidarity versus new opportunity are not independently documented at that level of precision. West and Zampella founded Respawn Entertainment in April 2010 with Electronic Arts as a partner; the studio's then-unannounced project was revealed in 2013 as Titanfall.

A note on a fact absent from most retrospective coverage written before late 2025: Vince Zampella died on December 22, 2025, in a car crash on Angeles Crest Highway near Los Angeles, at age 55. At the time of his death he was CEO of Respawn Entertainment and had led EA's Battlefield franchise through the 2025 launch of Battlefield 6. This is reported consistently across CBS News, Deadline, NBC Los Angeles, GameSpot, and Video Games Chronicle. Any account of Call of Duty's founding that treats Zampella as an ongoing, present-tense figure in the industry is now out of date; this analysis notes his death because it materially changes how the 2010 chapter of this story should be read in 2026 — not as a rupture between two studios that both continued forward in parallel, but as the beginning of two separate paths, one of which recently ended.

Activision's institutional response to the 2010 crisis is the more durable structural story. Sledgehammer Games — founded July 21, 2009, by Glen Schofield and Michael Condrey, both veterans of EA Redwood Shores' Dead Space — had been developing an unrelated third-person shooter when Activision redirected the studio to co-develop Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 3 (2011) alongside the reconstituted Infinity Ward. It was the first mainline release credited to two studios simultaneously. Treyarch, meanwhile, delivered Call of Duty: Black Ops (2010) independently, which passed $1 billion in worldwide sales and set opening-day revenue records (approximately $360 million in North America and the UK alone) — evidence that the franchise's commercial strength did not depend on any single studio's leadership continuity.

INTERPRETATION: The 2010 crisis is best read as the forcing event for a structural shift Activision may well have pursued eventually regardless — a permanent three-studio rotation (Infinity Ward, Treyarch, Sledgehammer Games, later joined by support studios including Raven Software and Beenox) replacing single-studio reliance. The available evidence supports that the crisis accelerated this shift; it does not establish that the rotation was the only or inevitable response, and Activision has not published an internal account confirming this as deliberate crisis-driven strategy rather than incremental necessity.

4. Case Study: Engine Lineage and the Path to a Unified Codebase

Call of Duty's technical lineage is a genuinely interesting case study in how competing internal codebases eventually converge — but the version history requires correction from commonly circulated summaries, including the one this analysis started from.

The original Call of Duty (2003) ran on a modified id Tech 3 (originally built for Quake III Arena, 1999), incorporating Ritual Entertainment's ÜberTools. Infinity Ward's subsequent internal fork became the "IW engine," versioned roughly as follows, per the consolidated version history:

Version Debut Title (Year) Studio
IW 1.0 Call of Duty (2003) Infinity Ward
IW 2.0 Call of Duty 2 (2005) Infinity Ward
IW 3.0 Call of Duty 4: Modern Warfare (2007) Infinity Ward
IW 4.0 Modern Warfare 2 (2009) Infinity Ward
IW 5.0 Modern Warfare 3 (2011) Infinity Ward
IW 6.0 Call of Duty: Ghosts (2013) Infinity Ward
IW 7.0 Infinite Warfare (2016) Infinity Ward
IW 8.0 Modern Warfare / Warzone (2019–2020) Infinity Ward + Infinity Ward Poland
IW 9.0 Modern Warfare II (2022) Infinity Ward, Treyarch, Sledgehammer Games (unified)

The most consequential correction here concerns IW 9.0. It is commonly (and incorrectly) reported — including in the draft this analysis was built from — that the unified engine debuted with Modern Warfare III (2023) or Black Ops 6 (2024). The consolidated version record instead places its debut a full release cycle earlier, with Modern Warfare II in 2022. Modern Warfare III and Black Ops 6 continued on IW 9.0; they did not introduce it. This is not a minor date quibble — it changes the timeline of when Activision's three core studios actually began sharing a single engine foundation, which is the central technical claim of this section.

Treyarch's engine history ran on a separate track for most of the 2000s (the "Treyarch NGL" id Tech 3 fork) before adopting IW 3.0 for Call of Duty: World at War (2008) and modifying it substantially across the Black Ops sub-series. Sledgehammer's engine history is less cleanly documented than popular summaries suggest: one Tier 2 source describes the Advanced Warfare (2014) engine as built largely from scratch by Sledgehammer's own team rather than derived from Infinity Ward's IW 5.0 codebase, as is sometimes claimed. Given conflicting and thin sourcing on this specific point, this analysis treats Sledgehammer's engine lineage before its 2019 transition to IW 8.0 as independently developed, without asserting a specific numbered ancestor.

Infinity Ward Poland, founded and announced December 15, 2017, in Kraków, contributed substantially to IW 8.0's photogrammetry, volumetric lighting, and ray-tracing systems for the 2019 Modern Warfare. Michal Drobot's name is associated with the studio's rendering research through public talks and professional profiles, but his specific formal title (commonly reported as "director") was not independently confirmed against a primary source in this review and is presented here with that caveat.

By Black Ops 6 (2024), Treyarch's "Omnimovement" system — allowing sprinting, sliding, and diving in any direction — shipped inside the shared IW 9.0 codebase, an example of a design feature built on infrastructure two other studios had already stabilized two years earlier.

5. Case Study: Esports Economics and the 2024 Franchise Reset

The Call of Duty League illustrates a distinct pattern: an aggressive capital-intensive franchise model that required a public correction within four years of launch.

Activision and Major League Gaming launched the CDL in 2020 as a city-based franchise league, evolving from the Call of Duty World League (2016–2019). Twelve franchise slots were sold at a reported $25 million entry fee each, based on reporting from ESPN and Engadget ahead of the 2020 season. This was a substantial capital commitment for esports organizations, made during a period of aggressive valuation growth across franchised esports broadly, not a Call of Duty-specific phenomenon.

On April 16, 2024, CDL General Manager Daniel Tsay announced a comprehensive restructuring: all outstanding franchise fee obligations were eliminated, and the roughly $25 million in fees already collected was refunded to team owners. In exchange, the league increased teams' direct revenue share from in-game purchases — specifically CDL Team Packs and Champs bundle sales inside Modern Warfare III and Warzone — added a two-year minimum revenue guarantee, and expanded subsidies for teams hosting in-person Majors and Championships.

INTERPRETATION, not confirmed causal claim: The available sources document this restructuring but do not establish a single confirmed cause. Multiple plausible, non-exclusive factors align with the timing: broader esports-sector valuation corrections happening industry-wide in 2023–2024, high fixed costs of city-hosted live events, and shifting platform economics around streaming rights. Tsay's own framing was that the league was "redoubling efforts on the business side" — a statement consistent with a financial correction but not a specific admission of which factor was decisive. This analysis does not assert that the franchise-fee model "failed" in a specific documented sense; it presents the restructuring as evidence consistent with the model proving unsustainable in its original form, while noting that Activision has not published a detailed account of the decision's internal reasoning.

6. Corporate Consolidation: Vivendi, Independence, and Microsoft

Activision Blizzard's ownership structure changed twice at a scale relevant to understanding who actually controlled Call of Duty at different points in its history.

In July 2008, Vivendi Games merged with Activision, Inc. to form Activision Blizzard, with Vivendi holding 54% of outstanding shares (52% on a fully diluted basis) — a controlling position. On July 25, 2013, Activision Blizzard itself repurchased 429 million shares from Vivendi for $5.83 billion, reducing Vivendi's stake to 11.8%; a separate investor group led by then-CEO Bobby Kotick purchased additional shares in the same transaction, bringing the total deal commonly reported at $8.2 billion. The buyback closed October 12, 2013, ending Vivendi's controlling position and returning Activision Blizzard to independent public-company status.

Microsoft announced its intent to acquire Activision Blizzard on January 18, 2022, at $95.00 per share in an all-cash transaction valued at $68.7 billion. This is the figure consistently reported across every primary and secondary source reviewed for this analysis — CBS News, TechCrunch, Forbes, Bloomberg, Variety, and Wikipedia's consolidated regulatory-filing summary — both at announcement and at the deal's October 13, 2023 close, where later coverage rounds it to approximately $69 billion. A frequently circulated figure claiming a "$75.4 billion final transaction cost" — the figure this analysis's source draft repeated — could not be corroborated against any source located during this review and has been removed rather than restated as fact, consistent with the evidence-gap principle that undocumented claims should be dropped, not filled in.

Call of Duty was the deal's central regulatory battleground across three jurisdictions:

  • United States: The FTC formally challenged the merger and sought a preliminary injunction to pause it; U.S. District Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley denied that request on July 11, 2023, and a Ninth Circuit emergency appeal was also denied days later, clearing the deal to close. The FTC's separate administrative complaint continued afterward and was ultimately dismissed by May 2025.
  • European Commission: Approved the deal on May 15, 2023, conditioned on ten-year licensing commitments covering cloud gaming services, including Nvidia's GeForce Now.
  • United Kingdom (CMA): Initially blocked the deal on April 26, 2023, citing cloud-gaming market dominance concerns. Microsoft restructured the transaction, divesting non-European cloud streaming rights for current and future Activision Blizzard titles to Ubisoft for 15 years; the CMA provisionally approved the revised structure September 22, 2023, and formally approved it October 13, 2023 — the same day the acquisition closed.

Microsoft separately committed to ten-year Call of Duty content-and-feature parity on PlayStation (signed July 16, 2023) and a ten-year commitment to bring the franchise to Nintendo platforms (December 2022), commitments made explicitly to satisfy regulators rather than as unprompted platform strategy.

The strategic payoff of the acquisition is visible in Call of Duty: Black Ops 6 (October 25, 2024), the first mainline entry to launch day-one on Xbox Game Pass. Bloomberg later reported, via a former Microsoft employee, that console and PC Call of Duty sales were approximately $300 million lower in 2024 than in 2023 — a figure worth treating carefully: it is a single-sourced insider disclosure relayed through Bloomberg and picked up by GameSpot, TheGamer, and Kotaku, not an official Microsoft or Activision financial disclosure. It is plausible and multiply reported, but it is not confirmed company guidance, and this analysis presents it at that confidence level rather than as an established internal projection.

7. Business Metrics: What the Numbers Actually Support

Two headline figures anchor most discussions of Call of Duty's commercial scale, and they carry different confidence levels.

Lifetime unit sales, "exceeding 500 million": This is well supported. Multiple outlets — Pure Xbox, Game World Observer, KitGuru, VGChartz — reported the franchise crossing 500 million units around Black Ops 6's October 2024 launch, sourced to statements relayed through Xbox/Microsoft channels. Confidence: strong.

Lifetime revenue, "$30 billion": This figure traces to a 2022 Washington Post report and is the last cumulative total with reasonably solid sourcing. Neither Activision nor Microsoft has published an updated official lifetime total since. Independent, unofficial analyst tallies from 2025 — built from a patchwork of individual title revenue disclosures, executive LinkedIn profile data, and social-media "sleuth" estimates — put the figure above $35 billion. Confidence: moderate on $30 billion as a dated but sourced figure; low on any more recent number, because none of the higher estimates reviewed trace to an official disclosure.

8. Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations

The strongest challenge to this analysis's framing is that treating the tri-studio rotation, the unified engine, and the esports restructuring as sequential "lessons learned" risks imposing a tidier narrative than the evidence supports. An alternative reading: Activision is a large publisher managing a portfolio of studios and a mature annual franchise, and structural adjustments — bringing in Sledgehammer, consolidating engines, restructuring esports economics — may simply reflect ordinary large-company operational management rather than responses to specific crises. Under this reading, the 2010 Infinity Ward crisis and the 2024 CDL restructuring are not obviously causally connected events in a single throughline; they are two separate corrections separated by fourteen years, and connecting them into one "institutional adaptability" narrative — as section 3's interpretation above does — may overstate the coherence of Activision's decision-making across a decade and a half and multiple changes in corporate ownership (Vivendi era, independent era, Microsoft era).

A second, narrower challenge concerns causality in the engine-consolidation story. This analysis frames IW 9.0 as resolving "engine fragmentation" — but the available sources document that the three studios began sharing an engine; they do not establish, from a developer statement or technical postmortem, that fragmentation itself was a significant production problem the unification was designed to fix, as opposed to a standard mid-generation technical refresh that happened to be shared across studios for efficiency reasons unrelated to any prior crisis. The Evidence Gap Rule applies directly here: sources document the change but not a stated reason, and this analysis has accordingly avoided asserting "X was designed to solve Y" language in section 4.

A third challenge concerns the framing of Microsoft's Game Pass strategy as a deliberate trade of retail revenue for subscription value. The $300 million figure, even if accurate, describes an outcome, not a documented strategic intention. It is equally possible that Microsoft underestimated the cannibalization effect rather than knowingly accepting it as a calculated trade-off; the available public reporting does not include an internal Microsoft strategy document confirming intent, only the after-the-fact sales impact.

9. Limitations

This analysis relies substantially on Wikipedia's consolidated summaries for several sections (engine version history, the Microsoft acquisition timeline, corporate ownership changes), which are themselves secondary aggregations of primary reporting rather than primary sources directly reviewed here. Where a claim could be traced to identifiable original reporting (court documents, press releases, named-outlet journalism), that is noted; where it could not, the claim is flagged at reduced confidence rather than presented as settled. The 007: Nightfire origin story rests on a single retrospective interview and should be understood as testimony from a credible participant, not as independently corroborated business history. Sledgehammer's pre-2019 engine lineage remains genuinely unclear from the sources reviewed and is presented with that uncertainty preserved rather than resolved. Financial figures more recent than 2022 (lifetime revenue above $30B, the $300M Game Pass impact) are unofficial and should not be cited as company-confirmed totals.

10. Conclusion

What we know: Call of Duty's studio structure was reshaped by a documented, dated leadership crisis at Infinity Ward in 2010, resolved through a tri-studio production model that persists in modified form today. Its technical foundation moved from a modified id Tech 3 through nine numbered generations of an internal engine to a genuinely unified codebase shared by all three core studios, a convergence that began with Modern Warfare II in 2022, not the later titles commonly credited with it. Its esports league sold franchise slots at $25 million each in 2020 and reversed that entire model by 2024. Its parent company changed hands twice at large scale — first losing independence to Vivendi in 2008, regaining it in 2013, then being acquired outright by Microsoft for $68.7 billion in a deal that closed in October 2023 after contested review in three major regulatory jurisdictions.

What we think: The available evidence is consistent with — though does not conclusively prove — a reading in which Activision's repeated structural corrections (multi-studio rotation, engine unification, esports economics) reflect a pattern of reactive institutional adaptation rather than a single coherent long-term strategy authored from the start. This is an interpretation, not a confirmed fact, and the counterarguments above should be weighed alongside it.

What we don't know: Why Activision's leadership specifically framed the 2010 crisis as requiring a permanent three-studio model rather than a temporary fix (no internal account has been published); the precise causal driver behind the 2024 CDL restructuring beyond the league's own general statements; Sledgehammer's actual engine ancestry before 2019; and any officially confirmed lifetime revenue figure more recent than the 2022 $30 billion report. Where sources documented an event without explaining its cause, this analysis has said so rather than supplying an explanation the record does not support.
