# The Live-Service Trap: Why Publishers Keep Funding Games-as-a-Service Despite Catastrophic Failures

*By the Game Research Writer — fact-checked against a 35-claim ledger — September 2026*

In August 2024, Sony took the multiplayer shooter *Concord* offline fourteen days after launch and refunded everyone who bought it. Firewalk Studios, the developer, closed within two months. Around the same time, Warner Bros. Discovery told investors that *Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League* had produced a $187 million impairment charge and dragged games-division revenue down 41% in a single quarter. A year earlier, Sega cancelled *Hyenas* — reportedly its most expensive game ever — during its own open beta, before it shipped at all.

These are not isolated accidents. They are three data points in a pattern that includes Sony's own retreat from 12 planned live-service titles to 6, years of turmoil at BioWare over *Dragon Age: The Veilguard*, and the closure of Arkane Austin after *Redfall*. The central question this piece investigates: given this track record, why do major publishers keep greenlighting games-as-a-service (GaaS) projects? The evidence points to a structural answer rooted in capital markets, market concentration, and development timelines — not simple executive error. But some numbers commonly cited to make this case, including in an earlier draft of this analysis, don't hold up under verification. Where that's true, this piece corrects them and says so.

**A note on sources:** Several widely repeated figures in live-service reporting are journalist or analyst estimates, not disclosures from the companies involved, because Epic Games, Take-Two, and miHoYo do not publish detailed per-title revenue. Every estimate below is labeled as such. Full sourcing is in the companion claim ledger.

## Why Capital Markets Favor Live Service
*Strong inference.* Publicly traded publishers are valued on predictability of cash flow as much as on total revenue. A successful live-service title behaves like a subscription business — recurring revenue via microtransactions, battle passes, and expansions — which tends to command higher valuation multiples than a hits-driven release slate. This is an interpretation of publisher behavior, not something any single executive has confirmed in these words, but it's consistent with public statements from platform holders about wanting "sustainable" revenue, and with continued investment even after Concord's failure was already public.

## The Outliers That Keep the Thesis Alive
*Confirmed, with important caveats.* A small number of live-service titles generate enough revenue to make the whole category look viable — Fortnite, GTA Online, and Genshin Impact. But the precision commonly attached to their revenue overstates what's actually known:

- **Fortnite:** Epic disclosed $24.8B lifetime gross through 2022 in court filings (Epic v. Apple) — confirmed. Since then, Epic has not disclosed figures; annual-revenue estimates range $5.2B–$6B, and lifetime-total estimates range from $23B to over $40B depending on methodology.
- **GTA Online:** GTA V's 225–230M lifetime units and Take-Two's 78–82% recurrent-spending share of net bookings are official disclosures. GTA Online's own revenue (~$5B lifetime, commonly cited) is an analyst estimate — Take-Two does not break it out.
- **Genshin Impact:** HoYoverse's total company revenue was ~$4.7B in 2024, down 23% year over year (Niko Partners via Bloomberg) — a declining trajectory, not a steady cash cow. The commonly cited "$10B+ lifetime player spending" figure is a Sensor Tower estimate, not a miHoYo disclosure.

## Market Saturation and the Concentration of Playtime
*Strong inference, with a correction.* Newzoo's reports are the standard source for the claim that new games struggle because playtime concentrates in older, entrenched titles. The widely cited "60–61% of playtime goes to games 6+ years old" figure comes from Newzoo's 2024 report (2023 data). Newzoo's own 2025 report revised that same figure down to 55%. The underlying pattern — that the top 20 titles still capture over 50% of playtime, and new releases have held steady at roughly 12–13% for several years — remains supported, but the specific "60%" figure repeated across the industry is outdated by Newzoo's own later methodology.

## Three Case Studies

**Concord.** Confirmed: launched Aug 23, 2024 at $39.99 in a free-to-play-dominated genre; peaked at 697 concurrent Steam players; pulled offline with full refunds Sept 6, 2024 (14 days); Firewalk Studios closed about a month later. The often-cited "$400 million total cost" traces to one disputed secondary report; the better-sourced figure is a ~$200M development-deal estimate that excludes acquisition and marketing costs.

**Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League.** Confirmed: Rocksteady (previously known for the single-player Batman: Arkham trilogy) began work around 2016–2017; the game shipped Feb 2024 after two delays. WBD's CFO confirmed a $200M EBITDA hit; the SEC 10-Q specifies a $187M impairment. Games revenue fell 41% year over year the following quarter. Reported peak: 320 concurrent Steam players during a free weekend, against an internal expectation of 100,000 (Eurogamer reporting).

**Hyenas.** Confirmed: cancelled September 2023 after roughly six years in development, during open beta. Multiple outlets (citing anonymous VGC sources) report it was Sega's highest-budget game ever, exceeding Shenmue's reported ~$70M budget. **Correction from the original draft:** no source establishes a specific total cost for Hyenas. The frequently cited "$100 million loss" figure does not appear in any reviewed reporting and has been removed.

## Why Legacy Studios Struggle With the Pivot
*Strong inference, with contested causal detail.* BioWare's Dragon Age: The Veilguard was rebuilt in 2017 around a multiplayer, Anthem-codebase structure (codename "Morrison"), then reverted to single-player in Feb 2021. Why the pivot happened is genuinely contested in the sourcing: some reporting frames it as an EA mandate; BioWare veteran Mark Darrah has said publicly it was partly an internal rationalization for moving staff onto Anthem. Both appear in credible reporting, and this piece doesn't resolve which is more accurate. Rocksteady and Arkane Austin both saw senior creative departures around their live-service projects — well documented — but the specific causal claim that "live-service friction" drove those departures, as opposed to the ordinary pressures of any troubled project, is weaker and treated here as suggestive context only.

## Sony's Portfolio Retreat
Confirmed: Sony acquired Bungie for $3.6B (Jan 2022 announcement). By November 2023, Sony's earnings call revealed only 6 of 12 planned live-service titles remained on track for FY2025 (March 2026); The Last of Us multiplayer, and projects at Bend Studio and Bluepoint Games, were all cancelled. Jim Ryan retired March 2024; Nishino and Hulst became co-CEOs that June. A lower-confidence addition: 2026 reporting suggests Sony wrote down Bungie's value by ~$765M and is winding down Destiny 2 live support — sourced to a lower-tier aggregator cross-referenced against a Wikipedia timeline, not a primary filing reviewed directly here.

## Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations
- **Survivorship bias:** this piece, like most coverage, centers on the most visible flops. Quiet live-service failures are underrepresented in available reporting.
- **Premium single-player games also fail**, just less visibly; this piece doesn't build an equivalent survey of single-player flops, so it can't make a rigorous risk comparison between the two models.
- **"Live service" isn't one category.** Fortnite (built live-service from day one) and Suicide Squad (retrofitted onto a premium release) fail or succeed for different structural reasons; grouping them risks flattening real differences.
- **The capital-markets explanation is an interpretation**, not a confirmed mechanism — no executive statement reviewed here explicitly cites valuation multiples as the reason for a specific greenlight decision.

## Limitations
Precise financials for non-disclosing companies (Epic, miHoYo, Take-Two on GTA Online specifically) don't exist in verifiable form — every figure for those titles here is a third-party estimate. Several claims about internal motivation rely on anonymous sourcing or contested first-person accounts. This piece covers mainly Western and Japanese publisher failures, not the Chinese live-service market. "Live service" is treated as one category for convenience, though it spans very different business models and risk profiles.

## Conclusion

**What we know:** Concord, Suicide Squad, and Hyenas were confirmed, documented failures with real financial consequences — a $187M impairment (confirmed via SEC filing), a studio closure, and a pre-launch cancellation. Sony's live-service roadmap was cut from 12 titles to 6. A handful of outlier titles generate enough real revenue, even under conservative estimates, to keep the live-service thesis economically credible industry-wide.

**What we think:** Capital markets' preference for recurring revenue plausibly explains continued GaaS greenlighting, though unconfirmed for any specific project. Long development cycles (6–10 years) likely cause publishers to greenlight based on trends that have faded by launch. Forcing single-player studios into live-service production plausibly causes organizational friction, though the causal chain to specific departures is rarely confirmed in a single source.

**What we don't know:** The true industry-wide failure rate (quiet failures are undercounted). Precise revenue for Fortnite, GTA Online, or Genshin Impact — only competing estimate ranges exist. Whether long development cycles specifically predict failure, since this piece didn't compare failure/success rates by development length. Whether the capital-markets incentive actually drives individual greenlight decisions, absent internal company documents.
