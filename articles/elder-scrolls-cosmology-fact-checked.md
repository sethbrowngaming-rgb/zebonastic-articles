# The Architecture of an Unreliable Universe: Cosmology and Historiography in The Elder Scrolls

*A Game Research Writer analysis. Compiled and fact-checked August 2026.*

![Original illustrative artwork: the Aurbis Wheel, seen edge-on, is the Tower — Anu's stasis and Padomay's change in permanent tension](images/hero_aurbis_wheel.png)
*Illustrative artwork produced for this piece, not a game screenshot or Bethesda asset.*

---

## 1. Introduction

The Elder Scrolls is unusual among fantasy settings in that its writers built unreliability into the cosmology itself, on purpose. This piece asks one question: **how does the setting's cosmological architecture actually work, and how much of what circulates as "Elder Scrolls lore" is confirmed in-universe text versus fan-synthesized systematization of scattered, contradictory sources?**

That second half matters because an earlier draft of this analysis — built from a general pass over the setting's mythology — presented several structures with more confidence than the underlying sources support. A table of cultural creation myths misattributed the Dunmeri Tribunal and the dragon Alduin to Nord cosmology and softened a tragic Khajiit myth into a story about love. A table of mythic "Enantiomorph" iterations stated a causal outcome (Trinimac's transformation into Malacath) that actually belongs to a separate myth centuries removed from the event it was attached to. The Warp in the West was described with six factions and a simple annexation outcome, where primary sources document seven factions and a genuinely paradoxical result. Those corrections are made explicitly below, per [[claude/elder-scrolls-cosmology-claim-ledger.md|the accompanying claim ledger]], rather than silently folded in.

## 2. Context: Why This Cosmology Behaves Differently From Other Fictional Universes

Most constructed fantasy cosmologies are written from a single authorial vantage point: even when in-world texts disagree, the reader is usually given enough narrative signal to know which account is "true." The Elder Scrolls does the opposite by design. Former Bethesda writer Michael Kirkbride has described the founding commitment as being "to never release anything into the world that wasn't written by somebody that lived there. The voice of the author should not come in there, and dictate what's true or not." Bethesda's Loremaster has separately confirmed that the resulting interpretive freedom for the community is "absolutely by design," not an accident of scale (PC Gamer, 2023–2024 reporting on Elder Scrolls canon policy).

**OBSERVATION:** No centralized, omniscient lore document exists; the setting's cosmology is assembled entirely from in-universe books, sermons, and interviews attributed to named (and frequently biased) authors.
**INTERPRETATION:** This is a deliberate authorial choice, not an emergent side effect of a long-running franchise having many writers over time.
**CONFIDENCE:** We know this from direct statements by the people who built the system.

This context matters for everything that follows: a claim like "Lorkhan was a trickster" is true of the Aldmeri textual tradition, not true of the setting as a settled fact, and a claim about CHIM is true of what Vivec's sermons say, not necessarily true within the fiction Vivec himself inhabits — because Vivec is explicitly written as an unreliable narrator even by in-universe standards.

## 3. Evidence: The Cosmological Architecture

### 3.1 From the Godhead to the Aurbis

At the root of the cosmology sits the Godhead — an unmanifested, dreaming consciousness whose "Dream" the entire universe is said to constitute. Within that Dream, the first division is between Anu (Stasis, "IS") and Padomay (Change, "IS NOT"); their interaction produces the Aurbis, the totality of the cosmos. Because pure Anu and pure Padomay are too abstract to interact directly, each differentiates into a "soul" — Anui-El from Anu, Sithis from Padomay — and it is the tension between these two that produces the Original Spirits, the et'Ada, of which Akatosh (the god of Time, called Auri-El by the Aldmer) is traditionally the first to take stable form.

**CONFIDENCE:** Strong inference. This structure is drawn consistently across multiple in-game texts (*The Monomyth*, *The Annotated Anuad*, and related creation compilations) and is not seriously contested within the fandom's source-checking community, but it remains a cosmology described entirely by in-universe theologians rather than confirmed by any neutral outside narrator — there is no neutral outside narrator in this setting by construction.

### 3.2 Three cultures, three creation stories

| Cultural Tradition | Primordial Framing | View of Creation | Role of Lorkhan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Altmer (High Elf) | Anu / Padomay via Anui-El / Sithis | Tragic fall; loss of original divine status | Trickster who bound divine spirits to mortality |
| Imperial (Cyrodilic) | Anu / Padomay via Anui-El / Sithis | Sacrifice framed as the origin of mortal potential | Architect (Shezarr) who fostered a new realm |
| Nord (Atmoran) | Shor within a cyclical Atmoran cosmology; Alduin the World-Eater as a separate destroyer/renewer figure, not a primordial pair | Cyclical destruction and renewal across "kalpas" (ages) | Shor's heart is cast down and forms Red Mountain directly — a parallel account with no Trinimac or Convention |
| Khajiit | Ahnurr / Fadomai as primordial parents; Lorkhaj as their child | Tragic: Lorkhaj tricks the other spirits into a scheme that gets several of them killed while building a home for Fadomai's daughter Nirni; he is cursed for it | Trickster-creator, punished by exile into a cursed lunar cycle — closer in tone to the Altmer account than to a story about love |

The original draft's Nord row conflated the Dunmeri Tribunal ("Almsivi") and the dragon Alduin with Nord primordial cosmology; neither belongs there. Its Khajiit row inverted the myth's tone from tragic-trickster to loving-creator. Both are corrected above. **What survives from the original claim is the more interesting point: every culture agrees a dualistic force pair drives creation, and every culture's moral read on the resulting sacrifice diverges sharply — including, once corrected, both the Nord and Khajiit versions**, which strengthens rather than weakens the draft's underlying thesis about historiographical fragmentation.

**CONFIDENCE:** Strong inference for the Altmer/Imperial rows (multiple corroborating in-game texts); Strong inference for the corrected Nord/Khajiit rows (cross-checked against independent lore references during this pass, not re-derived from a single primary source read cover to cover).

### 3.3 The three-way fracture: Aedra, Daedra, Magna Ge

| Group | Etymology | Relationship to Mundus's Creation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aedra | "Our Ancestors" (Aldmeri) | Sacrificed divinity to become the physical laws and bodies of reality | Bound as "Earth Bones" and planetary bodies |
| Daedra | "Not Our Ancestors" (Aldmeri) | Refused to participate; kept full power | Sovereign rulers of Oblivion's planes |
| Magna Ge | "Children of Magnus" | Began, then abandoned, creation | Fled to Aetherius; created the Sun and stars |

![The three-way fracture of the et'Ada into Aedra, Daedra, and Magna Ge, and what each group kept or gave up](charts/02_divine_fracture.png)

**CONFIDENCE:** Strong inference. This three-way taxonomy is one of the more consistently repeated structures across primary texts and is treated as close to settled within the fandom's own source-checking culture; it is presented here as Strong inference rather than "Confirmed" only because, as throughout this setting, every account of it still comes from an in-universe author with a point of view.

### 3.4 The Towers: reality's physical anchors

| Tower | Location | Builder | Stone | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adamantine Tower | Balfiera, High Rock | Aedra (Akatosh) | Zero Stone | Active |
| Red Tower (Red Mountain) | Vvardenfell, Morrowind | Aedra (Auri-El / Trinimac, Aldmeri account) | Heart of Lorkhan | Deactivated (3E) |
| White-Gold Tower | Cyrodiil | Ayleids | Chim-El Adabal (Amulet of Kings) | Deactivated/compromised (Oblivion Crisis) |
| Crystal Tower | Summerset Isles | Early Aldmer | Transparent Law | Destroyed |
| Walk-Brass (Numidium) | Mobile, Tamriel-wide | Dwemer | The Mantella | Destroyed/lost (Warp in the West) |
| Green-Sap (Graht-Oaks) | Valenwood | Bosmer | "Perchance Acorn" | Active, variable — a living Tower |
| Snow-Throat (Throat of the World) | Skyrim | Unknown / disputed | "The Cave" (speculative) | Described in-universe as sundered and kingless (speculative) |
| Orichalc | Yokuda (sunk) | Left-Handed Elves | A sword (speculative) | Destroyed with Yokuda's sinking (speculative) |

This table checks out well against independent lore compilations, including the "(Speculated)" flags the original draft already placed on Snow-Throat and Orichalc — one of the few places the original draft under-claimed rather than over-claimed. The one correction is Snow-Throat's builder, which independent sources treat as genuinely unknown rather than confidently "Early Nords or Mer."

![Successive collapse of the Towers across the eras, and what each collapse removed from the system](charts/03_towers_timeline.png)

**OBSERVATION:** Four of the eight tabulated Towers (Red Tower, White-Gold, Crystal Tower, Numidium) are confirmed inactive as of the setting's present.
**INTERPRETATION:** A pattern of progressive Tower deactivation across the Second, Third, and early Fourth Eras is visible in the historical record.
**THEORY:** If Towers function as literal structural anchors preventing Mundus from reverting to Oblivion/Aetherial chaos, sustained Tower loss should correlate with increasing metaphysical instability.
**CONFIDENCE:** The pattern itself (Towers going offline over time) is Strong inference — it is directly observable across documented events. The theory that this drives the setting toward apocalyptic collapse is Speculation: it is a popular fan reading, consistent with some in-game omens, but no primary text confirms this as the intended trajectory of the setting, and Bethesda has not stated it as authorial intent.
**ALTERNATIVE:** An equally consistent reading is that Tower "deactivation" is not cumulative decay but a recurring, survivable event type — the setting has already survived multiple such deactivations across thousands of years without the collapse this theory predicts, which is itself evidence against treating the trend as inexorable.

### 3.5 Dragon Breaks: when the Towers' timekeeper fails

Linear time is treated in-universe as a construct enforced by Akatosh rather than an inherent property of matter. When that enforcement is sufficiently disrupted, the result is a "Dragon Break": a period in which contradictory events are recorded as having happened simultaneously, later reconciled into one official history that preserves the contradictions rather than resolving them.

Three documented Dragon Breaks illustrate the mechanic at different scales:

- **The Battle of Red Mountain** (Merethic/First Era boundary): the Dwemer's activation of the original Numidium fractures the record of Nerevar's death, the Dwemer's disappearance, and the Tribunal's origin into multiple mutually exclusive accounts, each still told by a different modern faction (Ashlanders, Tribunal Temple, Nordic skalds, Imperial historians).
- **The Middle Dawn** (First Era): the Marukhati Selectives attempt to strip Akatosh of his Aldmeri aspects. In-universe sources give a specific duration — 1,008 years, an Elder Council figure — but that figure is itself presented within the fiction as possibly arbitrary and unreliable, which is a better illustration of the setting's unreliable-narrator premise than a clean round number would be.
- **The Warp in the West** (end of the Third Era): **seven** parties — Sentinel, Wayrest, Daggerfall, the necromancer Mannimarco, the Underking, Orsinium, and Emperor Uriel Septim VII with the Blades — simultaneously activate the Numidium in the Iliac Bay (the original draft said six). The outcome was not a clean Imperial annexation: roughly forty-four prior city-states consolidated into four surviving kingdoms (Daggerfall, Sentinel, Wayrest, Orsinium), each of which had, paradoxically, "achieved their goals to some degree," all swearing fealty to the Empire.

**CONFIDENCE:** The general Dragon Break mechanic and the Red Mountain and Middle Dawn summaries are Strong inference, well-corroborated across independent sources. The Warp in the West's faction count and outcome are corrected to Confirmed based on primary-source cross-checking during this pass.

## 4. Case Study: The Enantiomorph — How Confident Should We Be?

![Illustrative motif: King and Rebel as mirror images, with the Observer above paying the price of telling them apart](images/enantiomorph_motif.png)

![Confidence in the underlying event vs. confidence it is specifically an "Enantiomorph"](charts/04_enantiomorph_confidence.png)

### 4.1 Verified mechanics

The term "Enantiomorph" appears in exactly two places in all of published Elder Scrolls text: the title of Sermon 2 of the *36 Lessons of Vivec*, and a single line in an unofficial Kirkbride-adjacent interview text. Everything beyond that — the systematic "King/Rebel/Observer" taxonomy, and the claim that this pattern recurs as a metaphysical law across Tamrielic history — is fan lore scholarship built by cross-referencing scattered primary texts, not a framework Bethesda has laid out as a system anywhere in an official capacity.

### 4.2 Design analysis

Where the pattern does appear to recur — Anu/Padomay resolved by Nir; Lorkhan/Akatosh resolved (in the Aldmeri account) by Trinimac at the Convention; Dagoth Ur/the Nerevarine resolved by Vivec at the end of *Morrowind* — the structural similarity is genuinely striking and well documented at the level of *these specific events happening as described*. The claim that a single named mechanic called "the Enantiomorph" governs all of them, however, is a much thinner claim resting on far less textual support.

### 4.3 Limitations

One specific case in the original draft's Enantiomorph table conflated two unrelated myths: it listed Trinimac's Convention role (Observer) as resulting in his being "eaten by Boethiah and corrupted into Malacath." That transformation is a separate story, set in the Merethic Era after the Aldmer had already settled Tamriel, triggered by Boethiah's anger at Trinimac's anti-Velothi teachings — centuries removed from the Convention and causally unconnected to it. The role assignment (Trinimac as Observer at the Convention) is reasonably well attested; the outcome attached to it in the original table was not.

### 4.4 Counterarguments

The strongest objection to treating the Enantiomorph as a confirmed in-universe mechanic is source scarcity: a two-line textual footprint is a thin foundation for a five-event historical taxonomy. The community source consulted for this piece states plainly of its own reconstruction: "this is my own understanding of the idea, and not necessarily the whole truth." An equally valid alternative reading is that Bethesda's writers reused a "mirrored rivals resolved by a costly third party" story shape deliberately, as an authorial device across eras of writing, without that shape being a metaphysical law operating inside the fiction itself. Both readings are consistent with the available evidence; neither is ruled out by it.

**CONFIDENCE:** Weak inference to Speculation for the Enantiomorph as a named, systematic mechanic. Strong inference for the individual mythic events it describes, evaluated independently.

## 5. Case Study: CHIM, Zero-Summing, and Amaranth

![Illustrative motif: two outcomes of realizing the universe is a dream — an asserted self (CHIM) or an erased one (Zero-Summing)](images/chim_zerosumming_motif.png)

### 5.1 Verified mechanics

The *36 Lessons of Vivec* is a real, readable in-game book (first appearing in *Morrowind*) describing CHIM as an enlightenment state in which an individual, upon realizing the universe is a thought within the Godhead's Dream, asserts an unbreakable sense of self rather than being erased ("Zero-Summing"). Amaranth is described as the further step of an individual who has attained CHIM seeding an entirely new dreamed universe.

### 5.2 Developer statements

No Bethesda developer has confirmed CHIM, Zero-Summing, or Amaranth as objectively true events within the fiction, as opposed to the content of one narrator's (Vivec's) sermons. Bethesda's broader, confirmed design philosophy is precisely to avoid any single narrator — including Vivec — being treated as the arbiter of objective truth.

### 5.3 Design analysis

Much of the more systematized popular account of CHIM/Amaranth, including the text *C0DA*, was written by Michael Kirkbride after he left Bethesda and has functioned since as widely read, Bethesda-tolerated apocrypha rather than adopted canon.

### 5.4 Limitations

The claim that Tiber Septim used CHIM to retroactively alter Cyrodiil's climate from jungle (as depicted in 1994's *Arena*) to the temperate plains of later games is a specific piece of fan reasoning explaining a real production discontinuity between games made a decade-plus apart. No primary in-game text located during this pass states this as fact.

### 5.5 Counterarguments

The alternative explanation for the jungle-to-plains discontinuity is the mundane one: an early-1990s low-budget procedurally generated RPG's regional flavor text was simply revised by later, better-resourced sequels, with no in-fiction mechanism required or intended. The CHIM explanation is best understood as an in-universe rationalization invented by the fan community to reconcile two different decades of art direction, not as an event confirmed to have happened within the story.

**CONFIDENCE:** Confirmed that the *36 Lessons of Vivec* exists and describes CHIM/Zero-Summing this way. Strong inference to Speculation that these are "true" within the fiction rather than one narrator's contested cosmology. Speculation for the Tiber Septim/Cyrodiil-climate claim specifically.

## 6. Counterarguments and Alternative Interpretations

**The strongest opposing viewpoint:** treating any of this material — the Aurbis structure, the Towers, the Enantiomorph, CHIM — as a coherent "system" at all may itself be a category error. Bethesda's stated intent is closer to producing an unresolvable stack of competing in-universe testimonies than to producing a hidden, discoverable Truth for careful readers to reconstruct. Under that reading, a claim ledger like this one is useful for sorting *textual* claims (what does a given book say, and how well is that corroborated by other books) but cannot, even in principle, resolve *metaphysical* claims (what is actually true within the story), because the setting was built specifically so that question stays open.

**Supporting evidence:** the deliberate multiplicity of the Battle of Red Mountain's four mutually exclusive faction accounts, still unresolved after multiple subsequent games; the persistent in-universe scholarly feud between figures such as Phrastus of Elinhir and Lady Cinnabar of Taneth over matters of historical fact; Bethesda's own confirmed statement of intent to avoid an authorial arbiter of truth.

**Weaknesses in this article's approach:** confidence tiers like "Strong inference" and "Speculation" are borrowed from empirical research methodology and applied here to a constructed fiction; that mapping is a convenience, not a perfect fit, since a "well-corroborated" in-universe claim is corroborated only by other equally fictional, equally biased narrators, not by an external ground truth the way an empirical claim would be.

**Unresolved questions:** whether the Tower-collapse pattern documented in Section 3.4 is authorial foreshadowing of an eventual narrative climax for the franchise, coincidental accumulation of plot points across three decades of separately written games, or something the fandom has over-pattern-matched onto disconnected events, cannot be settled from currently available sources.

## 7. Limitations of This Analysis

This piece treats UESP and adjacent long-form fan lore scholarship (particularly the *Written in Uncertainty* project) as its primary secondary sources for cross-checking, because Bethesda has not published a centralized, authoritative lore compendium of its own. That means the "Tier 2" evidence used throughout is itself produced by an enthusiast community applying its own editorial judgment about which in-game texts to trust — a second layer of interpretation on top of an already unreliable-by-design set of primary sources. Several claims in the original draft (the Talos/Underking mechanics, the Arcturian Heresy's exact content, the Phrastus–Cinnabar feud's specifics) were judged consistent with existing knowledge during this pass but were not independently re-verified line-by-line against primary texts, given the scope of this piece; they are flagged in the accompanying claim ledger as carried at prior confidence rather than freshly confirmed.

## 8. Conclusion

**WHAT WE KNOW:** Bethesda deliberately built The Elder Scrolls' lore without a single authoritative narrator, using in-universe, biased texts as its only source of cosmology and history. The Aurbis/Aedra-Daedra-Magna Ge structure, the Towers-and-Stones system, and the mechanics of a Dragon Break are all consistently described across multiple independent in-game texts. The Warp in the West involved seven simultaneous factions, not six, and produced a paradoxical four-kingdom settlement rather than simple annexation. Several specific claims in the original draft — the Nord and Khajiit creation-myth rows, and one causal link in the Enantiomorph table — described the wrong entities or the wrong causal chain and have been corrected here.

**WHAT WE THINK:** The recurring "mirrored rivals plus a costly observer" pattern likely reflects a deliberate authorial motif reused across eras of writing, whether or not it constitutes a metaphysical law operating inside the fiction. The progressive deactivation of the Towers across the Second through early Fourth Eras is a real, documented historical trend; whether it represents the setting moving toward an eventual structural collapse is a popular and reasonable reading, not a confirmed authorial destination.

**WHAT WE DON'T KNOW:** Whether CHIM, Zero-Summing, and Amaranth are "true" events within the fiction, as opposed to the content of one contested narrator's sermons, is not resolvable from currently available sources — and, per Bethesda's own stated design philosophy, may not be a question the setting intends to answer at all. The same applies to whether the Enantiomorph is a real in-universe mechanic or a fan-constructed pattern layered onto independently written events.
