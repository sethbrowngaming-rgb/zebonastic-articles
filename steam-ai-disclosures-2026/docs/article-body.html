What 49.9% Counts: Reading Steam's AI Disclosure Numbers

Almost half of the games released on Steam in October 2026 carried an AI disclosure. The figure is documented. What it measures is narrower than the headlines made it sound.

1. Introduction

On November 2, 2026, a Reddit user posted a tally of the AI disclosure flag on Steam. The headline figure: 49.9% of games released in October 2026 disclosed AI-generated content, up from 1.7% in January 2024.[3][4] Coverage framed this as almost half of new Steam games using AI.[3]

The central question of this article is narrower: what does the 49.9% measure, and what can it support? The sources document the number, its source and much of its method. They do not establish how much AI is in those games, and they do not establish why the rate has risen.

OBSERVATIONThe figures come from a community analysis, not from Valve.[6] The analyst, who posts as u/ploz, says they run the game-browsing site PCGame.com, which carries the underlying statistics.[5] Where possible this article uses that site's own methodology page rather than press summaries.[2]

2. Context: what Valve asks developers to disclose

OBSERVATIONValve began requiring an AI disclosure in the Steamworks Content Survey in January 2024.[13] The survey separates "pre-generated" content, made with AI tools during development, from "live-generated" content, made while the game runs. Live-generated content carries an extra requirement: developers must describe the guardrails that stop the AI producing illegal content.[9][1]

OBSERVATIONValve's current survey page scopes the section to AI-created content that ships with the game and is consumed by players, with artwork, sound, narrative and localization as examples. It says efficiency gains from AI tools are not the focus.[1] PC Gamer reported on January 16, 2026 that Valve had rewritten the form this way, without removing it, and that coding helpers and similar tools fall outside the requirement. The change was first spotted by GameDiscoverCo's Simon Carless.[8]

INTERPRETATIONThe flag was therefore never an index of all AI use in game development, and since January 2026 it is narrower still. A game built with an AI coding assistant and no AI-made content for players has no reason to set it. The sources I found do not document how often Valve audits disclosures against what ships.

3. Evidence: what the tally shows

3.1 The headline numbers, and their units

OBSERVATIONThe analysis covers roughly 63,600 Steam releases since January 2024.[3] Disclosure is detected through Valve's AI flag, which developers set themselves when they submit a game. Undisclosed AI is not counted.[2] The reported figures are 1.7% in January 2024, 26.4% in October 2025 and 49.9% in October 2026.[3] On the analyst's statistics page, the yearly shares are 13.3% for 2024 (2,319 of 17,392 releases), 24.0% for 2025 (4,937 of 20,552) and 40.5% for 2026 through November 24 (12,351 of 30,474).[2]

Two cautions about reading Figure 1. First, the 49.9% is one month, the highest in the dataset;[3] the 2026 figure to date is 40.5%.[2] Second, the numbers move because the dataset is live. Early-November coverage gave 13.2% for 2024 and 40.1% for 2026 to that date,[3][4] and the statistics page later showed 13.3% and 40.5%.[2] Every figure here carries the date of the source it came from.

ARITHMETICFrom October 2025 to October 2026 the monthly rate rose 23.5 percentage points, from 26.4% to 49.9%, a factor of about 1.9. The January 2024 figure is a one-month starting point from the first weeks of the rule, so a "29-fold rise" computed from it would rest on a thin baseline. This article does not lean on it.

3.2 Does anyone else see the same direction?

OBSERVATIONYes. In July 2025, Totally Human Media's Ichiro Lambe counted 7,818 Steam titles carrying the flag, about 7% of the library, and a little under one in five of that year's releases to date.[10] That is a count of the same self-reported flag, taken a year earlier, and it is a stock of games rather than a monthly rate. It cannot be set beside the PCGame percentages as like for like. Table 1 lists this and the other figures that circulate under the label "AI use in games," and what each one counts.

INTERPRETATIONTwo separate analyses of the same flag agree that the count of flagged games climbed from 2024 into 2026. That supports the direction of the trend. Because both read a self-reported flag, their agreement does not show that the flag tracks real AI use. The evidence for direction is stronger than the evidence for any specific percentage.

3.3 Where the growth sits

ARITHMETICDisclosing releases rose from 2,319 in 2024 to 12,351 in 2026 so far, about 5.3 times. Releases without the flag rose from 15,073 to 18,123, about 20%, and the 2026 count is still a partial year.

INTERPRETATIONOn these counts the share rose mainly because the number of flagged releases multiplied, not because other releases fell away. This holds only if the analyst's release counts are comparable across years. The page says releases come from Steam's own list but does not explain how a release date is determined.[2]

4. Case study: the disclosure flag as a measuring instrument

4.1 Verified mechanics and Valve's stated scope

Valve's page establishes the mechanics: a survey section, two content categories, a guardrail requirement for live-generated content, and a pre-release review that treats AI-generated content like any other content.[1] It also states the scope discussed above. This is Valve's own description of what the form is for. It is not an audit of what developers submit.

4.2 What disclosing developers say the AI did

OBSERVATIONAmong 10,723 analyzed disclosures for 2026 releases, 63% named art, 32% text and translations, 27% store page and marketing, 22% music and sound, 17% code, 8% voices and 3% AI active during play. Eight percent of disclosures concerned store-page and marketing material only.[2] A program reads these categories from developers' own words, and vague statements are left out of every category.[2] A further 1,628 flagged games are in the totals but not in the use breakdown: 1,295 have store pages visible only to signed-in users, and 333 carry the flag but show no statement.[2]

INTERPRETATIONMost disclosures concern pre-made assets, not AI running inside the game. The 17% naming code is ambiguous. Valve's form excludes efficiency tools, and one outlet cautions that the code figure should be read carefully for that reason.[7] The sources do not say what the code disclosures describe.

4.3 Who discloses

OBSERVATIONOf 2026 releases with fewer than 10 reviews, 47% carry the flag, against 15% of those with 1,000 or more.[2] Developers with ten or more releases this year show a 64% rate against 38% for single-release developers, but the high-output group is only 62 developers.[2] Earlier coverage gave 68% and 37% for the same comparison, another sign that the figures move.[3] Flagged games are 41% of 2026 releases but 14% of their Steam reviews, and the page notes that reviews are a common stand-in for sales.[2] Among games with at least 50 reviews, positive-review rates are about 78% for flagged games against 83% for the rest.[2] The press summary notes this gap does not show that AI caused lower ratings.[6]

INTERPRETATIONOne reading fits the size pattern: small and high-volume releasers use generative tools as a production shortcut. One outlet makes the same suggestion while noting that the data do not show why these groups disclose more.[7] That reading is possible, not established. Section 5 sets out what else could produce the pattern.

5. Counterarguments and alternative interpretations

5.1 The strongest opposing view: the figure overstates AI use

The flag marks a declaration, not an amount. One disclosure can cover a single image, a translated string or one marketing asset, so 49.9% does not mean half of new games were made with AI.[7] The flag also covers marketing material, and 8% of 2026 disclosures concern store-page material only.[2] The tally is an independent community analysis, not Valve's data and not peer reviewed.[6][7] This view is well supported, and it is the main reason the headline reading is not supported.

5.2 The opposite view: the figure understates AI use

Undisclosed AI does not appear, and the analyst's own page says the figures are likely lower bounds.[2] Since January 2026 the flag also excludes AI tools used to speed up development.[1][8] Epic's Tim Sweeney argued that storefront labels make little sense because AI will be involved in nearly all future production.[8] GDC's 2026 survey found 36% of respondents using generative AI at work.[11] A trade summary of that survey lists research and brainstorming as the most common uses, followed by administrative work and coding help.[16] Research, brainstorming and administrative work would not ship to players, so a survey like this captures activity that the Steam flag excludes by design. The sources do not break the uses down far enough to quantify the overlap.

5.3 Two measures that do not move together

OBSERVATIONIn the sources I found, surveys of industry professionals do not show a rise like the Steam flag's. GDC reports 36% using generative AI at work,[11] and a separate survey, reported second-hand, found 29% in early 2026 against 36% a year earlier.[12] The same GDC survey found 52% of professionals saying generative AI is having a negative effect on the industry, up from 30% a year earlier and 18% the year before.[11]

INTERPRETATIONOne hypothesis is that the two populations differ: Steam's release flow is dominated by small releases, while a professional survey draws on people in the industry. Most 2026 releases are small: 19,176 of 30,474 have fewer than 10 reviews.[2] The sources do not describe who is missing from the survey, so this stays a hypothesis. A second hypothesis is that sentiment affects disclosure itself, in either direction: some developers may skip a label they expect players to dislike, others may treat it as routine. No source tests this.

5.4 A confound in the size pattern

INTERPRETATIONFigure 4 measures size by current review count.[2] Two effects could inflate the low-review bucket independent of developer size. First, games released in the most recent weeks have had the least time to collect reviews, and the most recent full month, October, has the highest monthly disclosure rate in the dataset.[3] Second, if players avoid flagged games, flagged games would sit in low-review buckets even if their developers were large. "Small games disclose more" and "disclosed games attract fewer reviews" may be one set of data read from two directions. No source separates them. This is reasoned from how the data were built, not from a test.

5.5 Weaknesses in this article's own argument

  • The core dataset is one analyst's tally. The Reddit post and the statistics page are one effort and do not corroborate each other.[5]
  • The "growth sits in flagged releases" reading in 3.3 depends on release counts that the page does not fully explain.[2]
  • The claim that the January 2026 narrowing would by itself lower disclosures is an inference from what the rule says. No source measures its effect.
  • The professional-survey comparison mixes populations, years and sampling methods.

5.6 Unresolved questions

  • What does the monthly series look like around January 2026? I did not find one.
  • How are release dates determined? The analyst's page does not say.[2]
  • Does Valve check disclosures against shipped content? The sources I found do not say.
  • Which January 2024 baseline is right? Most coverage gives 1.7%, one outlet gives 1.4%.[3][14]

6. Limitations

  • Access. The original Reddit post could not be fetched, so it is read through press coverage and the analyst's own pages. I did not re-run the analysis.
  • Moving data. The statistics page is dated November 24, 2026 and updates daily;[2] press figures date from November 2 and 3. Numbers will have changed by the time you read this.
  • Secondary reporting. The Totally Human Media study, the Omdia figure and several summaries of the PCGame analysis are read through press reports, not the original documents.[10][12]
  • Valve's page is a snapshot. It shows current wording. I did not compare earlier versions.
  • Conflicting tallies exist. Another outlet published materially different baselines from an unnamed tally, including 30% of 2026 releases by mid-July and 10.9% for January 2024.[15] I could not trace its source and did not use it. It shows that public tallies of this flag do not all agree.
  • Out of scope. Game quality, player reaction beyond review share, and any claim about which games used AI well or badly.

7. Conclusion

What we know. Valve's form asks about AI content that ships to players, not efficiency tools. A community tally built on Steam's flag reports 49.9% of October 2026 releases and 40.5% of 2026 releases to November 24 carrying it. The flag is self-reported. Flagged releases numbered 2,319, 4,937 and 12,351 across 2024, 2025 and 2026 to date.

What we think. Disclosed AI use among new releases rose substantially since 2024, since two separate tallies point the same direction. Art is the most commonly named use. The rise is concentrated among games with few reviews. The disclosed share is a lower bound for disclosed-category AI use, because undisclosed use is invisible.

What we don't know. How much of the rise is real adoption, more willingness to disclose, a changed release mix or the January 2026 rule change. How much AI any single game used. How accurate the analyst's release dates and use categories are. Whether disclosure lowers reviews or low-review games simply disclose more.
